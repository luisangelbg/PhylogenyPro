Parsimony and distances

The quick trees: the ones that come out in seconds and serve as a starting point for likelihood and as a contrast for everything else. A distance tree is not a weak hypothesis if the model that corrects the distances is the right one, and parsimony remains the right tool for morphology. This chapter explains both, their indices, how the support of a branch is measured and what a consensus says when there are many equally short trees.

4.1What quick trees are for

This block produces trees in seconds, and that makes them easy to look down on. It is not a good idea. They have three legitimate uses:

  • Starting point. The maximum likelihood search of Block 5 needs an initial tree, and the better it is, the less risk there is of getting stuck in a local optimum.
  • Contrast. If the distance tree, the parsimony tree and the likelihood tree agree on the main clades, that is information. If they disagree, so is that, and you need to understand why before publishing any of them.
  • The right tool for morphology. With discrete characters and few changes, parsimony is not a cheap approximation to likelihood: it is the method the discipline uses, and with good reason.
✦A distance tree is not a weak hypothesis

It is when the distances are poorly corrected, which is a different matter. With the right model, neighbor joining is a consistent estimator: with enough data it converges to the true tree. Its bad reputation comes from using it with uncorrected distances, which is what does fail as soon as there is saturation.

4.2Distance trees

The method consists of two decisions: how the distance between each pair of sequences is measured and how the tree is built from the matrix.

The distances

Table 4.1. The distances offered by the distance menu.
DistanceWhat it corrects and when to use it
uncorrected (p)The proportion of differing positions. Useful only to see how much the others correct; not for inference.
JC69Multiple changes, assuming all changes are equally probable.
K80Separates transitions from transversions. The reasonable minimum for DNA.
F81Allows unequal base frequencies.
TN93Unequal frequencies and two classes of transition. The most complete on the list.
LogDet (Lockhart)The only one that tolerates heterogeneous composition among sequences. If the χ² test of Block 3 came out badly, this is the distance to use.
paralinear (Lake)A close relative of LogDet, with the observed frequencies.
Poisson and KimuraFor proteins: the first without composition correction, the second with Kimura’s classic approximation.
iTwo names for two different formulas

What ape calls logdet is the distance of Lockhart et al. (1994); the formula with the observed frequencies is Lake’s paralinear distance. They are often confused because they share the nickname. Here they appear separately and under their own names, so that you can report which one was used.

Two checkboxes modify the calculation. gamma correction applies the gamma correction with the α estimated by Block 3, and complete deletion decides what to do with gaps: when checked, it removes every column that has any gap before computing any pair; when unchecked, each pair uses the columns that both have. With a lot of missing data, complete deletion can leave you with very few columns.

The methods

The method menu offers neighbor joining (NJ), BIONJ, UPGMA, WPGMA and minimum evolution.

!UPGMA assumes a molecular clock

And most data do not meet it. UPGMA produces an ultrametric tree—all tips at the same height—, which is correct for a clustering dendrogram and false for a phylogeny with unequal rates. Use it to see structure, not to infer relationships. NJ and BIONJ do not assume a clock.

Balanced minimum evolution looks for the tree whose total length, computed with the balanced averages of Desper and Gascuel (2002) on Pauplin’s (2000) criterion, is the smallest. It is a true optimality criterion, with NNI and SPR search from several starts, and not a mere clustering algorithm.

4.3Maximum parsimony

The tree that needs the fewest changes to explain the matrix. Transparent, free of model assumptions… and vulnerable for that very reason, as you saw in Lab 1 of Chapter 1: without a model that corrects for multiple changes, parsimony interprets convergence as kinship.

The controls

  • characters: unordered (Fitch), where any state can go to any other in one step; ordered (Sankoff), where the cost depends on the pair of states and an order can be imposed—useful in morphology, when a character has graded states—; or transversions weighted, which penalizes transversions more than transitions.
  • tv:ts weight: how much more a transversion costs. With 2:1, a transversion is worth two steps. It is parsimony’s way of acknowledging that transitions are more frequent.
  • swapping: NNI, SPR or TBR (Section 4.4).
  • starts: how many different addition orders are tried. Several starts that reach the same tree are the best sign that the search was sufficient.
  • max trees: how many equally short trees are kept. Morphological matrices sometimes produce thousands.

4.4The search: NNI, SPR and TBR

The number of topologies grows faster than factorially: with twenty taxa there are more than 10²⁰ unrooted trees. They cannot all be evaluated, so the search starts from one and tries rearrangements.

Table 4.2. The three types of rearrangement, from smallest to largest reach.
TypeWhat it doesWhen
NNISwaps the two groups adjacent to an internal branch. Small moves.Fast; easily gets stuck in local optima.
SPRPulls off a branch with everything hanging from it and regrafts it elsewhere.The usual balance between reach and time.
TBRCuts the tree in two and rejoins the halves through every possible pair of branches.The most exhaustive and the slowest.

No heuristic search guarantees the optimum. When two programs give different trees under the same criterion, it is usually this and not a disagreement of method: they are two local optima.

4.5The indices: CI, RI, RC and HI

Once the tree is in place, four indices describe how much homoplasy—convergence and reversal—it contains.

∑The four definitions

CI = m/s, the minimum possible number of changes over the number the tree needs. It equals 1 without homoplasy.
HI = 1 − CI, the homoplasy index: the same thing said the other way round.
RI = (g−s)/(g−m), where g is the length on the worst possible tree: how much of the similarity that could be synapomorphy actually is.
RC = CI × RI, the rescaled consistency index.

!The CI falls with the number of taxa

Systematically, and without homoplasy having increased. A CI of 0.8 with fifteen taxa and one of 0.4 with two hundred may describe the same matrix. That is why CIs are not compared between studies of different size, and why the RI, which corrects part of the problem, is the one worth reporting.

4.6Branch support

The sentence at the head of this section on screen deserves repeating: a clade without support is not a bug: it is a polytomy your data cannot resolve. The program offers three measures.

Bootstrap

The alignment columns are resampled with replacement, the tree is rebuilt hundreds of times and the percentage of replicates in which each branch appears is counted. It measures the consistency of the signal in the data, not the probability that the branch is correct. The convention: 70% for “well supported,” 95% for strong.

Jackknife

The same, but instead of resampling with replacement a fraction of the columns is deleted in each replicate. It gives results similar to the bootstrap and is somewhat more conservative.

Bremer support

Different in nature: how many extra steps have to be accepted before finding a tree in which that branch no longer appears. It is read in steps, not as a percentage, and it depends on the size of the matrix: in a large matrix, a Bremer of 5 may be little. A value of 1 means that one step is enough to undo the branch.

✕Believing that the bootstrap protects against systematic error

It does not, and it is important to understand this. The bootstrap measures whether the signal holds up when the same data are resampled under the same method. If the method is biased—long-branch attraction, heterogeneous composition—, the wrong signal is in all the replicates, and the bootstrap will support the wrong branch at 100%. With more data, it will support it more.

4.7Consensus and comparison

When the search returns several equally short trees, the question is what they agree on. The menu offers four consensus methods:

Table 4.3. The four consensus methods.
ConsensusKeeps
strictOnly the clades present in all trees. The most conservative and the one published by default.
majority rule (50%)The clades present in more than half, with their frequency noted.
semistrictThe clades that no tree contradicts, even if they are not in all of them. Useful when some trees lack taxa.
greedyAdds clades by frequency as long as they are compatible. The most resolved and the least conservative.

The polytomies of a strict consensus are the result: they say exactly where the data do not decide. A heavily collapsed consensus is not a failure of the analysis; it is an honest analysis of data that are not enough.

The How much the trees agree table compares by Robinson–Foulds distance everything the block has produced—distances, parsimony, consensus trees—to see at a glance which methods agree.

4.8The figure

The drawing uses the same engine that the tree studio of Block 12 will use: rectangular, circular or unrooted layout; cladogram to draw without lengths; support as numbers, coloured dots or none; and root as it is, midpoint or outgroup…, with multiple selection using Ctrl.

iAn unrooted tree has no ancestors

The methods in this block produce unrooted trees: they describe relationships, not direction. Placing the root is an additional decision, and with an outgroup it is a hypothesis that can be wrong. If you choose an outgroup that is not monophyletic in that tree, the program says so and leaves the tree as it was, instead of silently rooting it wrongly.

4.9How well it does, measured

Everything in this block was checked against ape and phangorn, and the results are in the validation folder. Three deserve mention:

  • The parsimony search reaches 1,916 steps on the example, the same optimum as phangorn’s pratchet, and thirteen steps better than optim.parsimony with SPR.
  • The balanced minimum evolution length reproduces that of ape::fastme.bal to within 5·10⁻⁹ on the same tree, so the formula agrees exactly.
  • With its own search from three starts, the program reaches a shorter tree (2.899076) than ape’s (2.901969). It is not that one of them is wrong: they are two local optima of the same criterion, and where a heuristic lands depends on where it starts.

4.10Frequent mistakes

✕Using uncorrected distances

It is what gave distance methods their bad reputation. With saturation, the p distance underestimates divergence exactly where it matters most, and the tree gets distorted. Always correct, and with the model that Block 3 chose.

✕Publishing a UPGMA tree as a phylogeny

UPGMA assumes a molecular clock. If your rates are unequal—and they almost always are—, the tree will be wrong in a systematic and predictable way: the fast lineages will come out more “derived” than they are.

✕Reporting a single shortest tree without saying how many there were

If the search found 4,800 equally short trees and you publish one, you are presenting as a result an arbitrary choice among 4,800. What is reported is the consensus, the number of optimal trees and their length.

✕Comparing the CI with that of another study

It is only comparable between matrices of the same size. Report the RI, and in any case say how many taxa and how many characters the matrix had.

✕Accepting the search with a single start

A single starting point and NNI is the recipe for getting stuck in a local optimum. Raise the starts, use SPR or TBR, and check that several starts reach the same length. If they do not, the search is not over.
