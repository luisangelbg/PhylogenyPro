I.1What is PhylogenyPro?

PhylogenyPro is an application that carries out a complete phylogenetic study inside the browser. It opens with a double click on a file, it is not installed, it asks for no account or license, and it does not send your data anywhere. On the other hand, it is not a viewer: it really computes. It aligns the sequences, chooses the substitution model, searches for the tree by parsimony, by distances, by maximum likelihood and by Bayesian inference, measures the support of each branch, tests the molecular clock, dates the nodes with fossils, fits diversification models, reconstructs ancestral characters and areas, compares trees that disagree and, at the end, writes the methods section with the numbers from your run and assembles a package with everything so that someone else can repeat it.

It is meant for anyone who needs to do a phylogenetic analysis properly and does not want—or cannot—set up a tower of command-line programs to get there. A graduate student with a first gene, a taxonomist who wants to date a radiation, a professor who has to teach maximum likelihood on Tuesday.

✦The sentence that sums up the program

A phylogenetic tree is not a datum: it is a hypothesis, and like every hypothesis it is worth what its assumptions are worth. This program is built so that the assumptions are in view at all times: which model was used, with what support, with which calibration, and what would have changed with another decision. Where something cannot be known, the screen says so instead of printing a round number.

It is the sister application of PopGeneticsPro, which covers what happens within a species—allele frequencies, structure, inbreeding. PhylogenyPro starts where that one ends: above the species level, where relationships are represented by a tree and time enters the story.

I.3Six ideas that hold up the whole manual

These six ideas come back again and again in the twelve chapters. If you read only two pages of this manual, make it these.

1 · The alignment is a hypothesis, and it is the first one

Aligning two columns means claiming that they descend from the same ancestral site. It is not a preparation step: it is the first hypothesis of the study, and all the others rest on it. A badly made alignment does not produce a tree with little support, which would be honest; it produces a firmly wrong tree, because misaligned columns are false informative sites and they enthusiastically support groupings that do not exist. That is why Block 2 has a viewer that lets you look at the alignment column by column, and why the trimming of ambiguous blocks comes with a preview of what is going to be removed.

2 · A site that changed twice shows only the last change

Almost all the difficulty of this discipline comes from there. The differences observed between two sequences underestimate the changes that really happened, and they do so more and more as divergence grows, until they level off: that is saturation. Correcting it is what substitution models do, and choosing the model well matters as much as choosing the inference method well.

3 · More data do not fix a wrong model

It is the most expensive mistake made in phylogenetics. When the model is misspecified, adding sites does not bring you closer to the correct answer: it increases confidence in the wrong one. Long-branch attraction is the classic case—with infinite data, parsimony converges more surely to the false tree—but the logic is general and also holds for the bootstrap and for the posterior probability. A 100% support on a bad model is 100% confidence in an error.

4 · “Support” means different things depending on who says it

A bootstrap of 70% and a posterior probability of 0.70 are not the same thing, and an ultrafast bootstrap of 90% is less support than a classical bootstrap of 90%. Each measure has its own scale and threshold, and confusing them is the most frequent mistake in this discipline. The program has a built-in interpretation aid that, next to each number, says which band of its own scale it falls in.

5 · Time does not come from the sequences

A maximum likelihood tree has branches measured in substitutions per site, not in years. The only thing that turns one into the other is an external calibration: a fossil, a published rate, a geological event. And a fossil almost never gives the age of a node: it gives its minimum age. That is why the dates of a study are no better than its worst calibration, and why no age is ever reported without its interval.

6 · Genes that disagree are a result, not a nuisance

When two genes give different trees, the temptation is to concatenate them and move on. But the disagreement may be the interesting part: incomplete lineage sorting, hybridization, chloroplast capture, introgression. Block 11 exists to measure that conflict instead of averaging it, and a branch with 100% bootstrap and 35% gene concordance is exactly the case you need to learn to see.

I.5What you need and how to open it

A modern browser—Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari, from the last few years—and nothing else. You need no internet, no installation and no administrator rights.

  1. Double-click index.html. It is the simplest way and it works for everything, because the program carries its examples embedded and does not look for any file on disk while it runs.
  2. Or with the local server, if you prefer to open it from another computer on the same network: right-click server.ps1 → Run with PowerShell, and then http://localhost:9800 in the browser. When run as administrator, it also prints the address for connecting from a tablet on the same Wi-Fi network.
!About speed

This is JavaScript, not C. One hundred maximum likelihood bootstrap replicates take minutes, and so do one hundred thousand generations of coupled Bayesian chains. The program estimates the time before starting, shows the progress and can be canceled. For phylogenomics with hundreds of taxa and millions of sites, keep using IQ-TREE or RAxML: the program’s home page says so in those very words, and with a table of sizes that were measured, not promised.

I.7The example data sets, and why they are simulated

The program comes with four example data sets, and all of them have a deliberate peculiarity: the sequences are simulated on a known tree, under a known model, with the gaps inserted lineage by lineage. The species names are real—Mexican plants, mostly of the genus Bursera, the copals—but the sequences are not those of any organism.

It is not a limitation: it is the reason they are good for learning. Each example carries its own correct answer. The true tree and the true alignment are stored alongside the data, so you can compare what you estimate with what really happened. That is impossible with real data, and it is exactly what you need when you are learning to tell a firm result from one that only looks firm.

I.8How a study is carried out from start to finish

The order of the blocks is not arbitrary: it is the order in which a real study is done, and each step uses what the previous one left behind.

  1. Load and align (Block 2). Declare which genome each partition comes from. Check the alignment by eye before going on; it is the only step that no algorithm does better than someone who knows the group.
  2. Look at the assumptions before choosing (Block 3): base composition and saturation. Then let the information criteria choose the model.
  3. Get a quick tree (Block 4) by distances and by parsimony. It serves as a starting point and as a contrast.
  4. Do the serious inference (Blocks 5 and 6): maximum likelihood with its support and, if time allows, Bayesian as well. That the two agree is valuable information; that they disagree, even more so.
  5. Add time (Block 7): root, clock test, calibrations, dates with their intervals.
  6. Ask the dated tree whatever you want to know: how the group diversified (8), how its characters evolved (9), where it was (10).
  7. Confront (Block 11): do your genes say the same thing? And your tree and the published one?
  8. Publish (Block 12): figure, methods and reproducible package.

Not every study goes through the twelve blocks, and it does not need to. A systematics paper may stop at 5; a biogeography one will need to go up to 10. The Block 12 report is written from what was actually run: if there was no clock, the report does not mention the clock.

I.10What the program does not do

An honest manual also says where its program ends. PhylogenyPro does not do any of this:

  • Large-scale phylogenomics. Hundreds of taxa and millions of sites are out of its reach because of speed, not method. The program’s home page has a table of sizes measured on an ordinary desktop computer.
  • Full multispecies coalescent. There is a species tree from quartets, which is a heuristic, not the exact ASTRAL algorithm nor a coalescent model with a likelihood.
  • Explicit phylogenetic networks with hybridization. There are split networks, which show the conflict without explaining it; there is no estimation of a number of hybridization events.
  • Annotation or sequence searching. It does not connect to GenBank or to any database: it has no network access, by design.
  • Deciding for you. The program computes, compares and warns; the interpretation, the choice of calibrations and the definition of the areas remain yours, and they are what you will have to defend before a reviewer.
✦Before moving on to Chapter 1

If some of the above seems overly cautious, it is worth remembering where the caution comes from. Molecular phylogenetics has spent forty years discovering that its firmest certainties—the 100% support, the date with three decimals, the ancestor reconstructed with confidence—were often artifacts of an assumption that nobody had checked. This program is written with that memory on its shoulders.

Almost every home page of a scientific program is a brochure: it says what the program does and invites you to begin. This one does something else. 1.3Lab 1: the Felsenstein zone

This is probably the most important corner of the whole program. It illustrates the result of Felsenstein (1978) that founded the modern critique of parsimony, and it does so by computing, not by telling.

What it shows

A tree of four taxa with the shape that does harm: two long branches that are not sisters, separated by short branches. With four taxa there are only three possible unrooted topologies, and with four possible states per site there are 256 patterns; but by symmetry those 256 reduce to the 15 partitions of the set {A, B, C, D}, so the probability of each pattern under Jukes–Cantor can be computed exactly, without simulating anything.

The panel uses that exactness to answer the question with infinite data: which informative pattern is the most probable? If the pattern that gathers the most probability is the one that groups the two long branches, then parsimony, given enough sites, will choose the wrong tree with complete certainty. That is what being inconsistent means: converging to the false answer.

The controls

  • Long branches and Short branches: the two lengths that define the scenario, in substitutions per site.
  • Sites: how many sites each simulated alignment has.
  • Replicates: how many alignments are simulated to measure how often each method gets it right.
  • ↻ New replicates: simulates again with another seed.

What to read

Three things, in this order:

  1. The true tree and Where parsimony fails: the panel computes by bisection the boundary of the scenario, that is, the exact point where the long branch becomes too long and parsimony starts getting it wrong. Moving the sliders to one side and the other of that boundary is the whole lesson.
  2. The three informative patterns and their exact probability. When the “long branches together” pattern overtakes the true pattern, that is it: parsimony has lost, however much data you give it.
  3. How often each method recovered the true tree, over the simulated replicates, with parsimony, with neighbor joining on Jukes–Cantor-corrected distances, and with maximum likelihood optimizing the branch lengths on each of the three topologies.
!The experiment you must do

Set up a scenario inside the zone—long branches of 0.6 and short ones of 0.03, for example—and then raise the sites from 100 to 2,000. Likelihood improves, as you would expect. Parsimony gets worse: with more data it is right less often, because each new site reinforces the false signal. That is the difference between a consistent method and one that is not, and it is idea 3 of the introduction seen live.

The “Why this matters in your own data” panel translates it: a genus with one fast-evolving species, a very distant outgroup or a saturated marker produce exactly this geometry without anyone having looked for it. If your tree joins the outgroup with the most divergent taxon, suspect this before you suspect the biology.

1.4Lab 2: saturation and distance correction

The second lab makes idea 2 visible: a site that changed several times shows only the last change.

Two sequences diverge under Kimura’s two-parameter model, with rates that vary among sites according to a gamma distribution. The panel plots the estimated distance against the true distance, with three overlaid curves: the raw differences (the proportion of differing positions), the model correction, and the line of the perfect estimator, which is the diagonal.

What you see is immediate: the raw differences start out stuck to the diagonal and peel away more and more, until they flatten out. That is saturation. The correction brings them back to the diagonal, but only as far as it has a solution; beyond that, the formula breaks down and the panel shows it.

The controls

  • Maximum divergence: how far the two sequences are allowed to drift apart.
  • ts/tv ratio: how many transitions there are for each transversion. Raise it and you will see that transitions saturate much earlier.
  • Γ shape: the inequality of rates among sites. Low values—a few very fast sites and many almost frozen—bring saturation forward.
  • Sites: how many positions each sequence has; with few, noise dominates.
  • rate variation among sites (+Γ): turns the heterogeneity off and on to see how much it changes.

The second plot, Transitions and transversions, is the one you will use with your own data in Block 3. The rule can be read straight off the screen: when the transition curve stops rising and starts to fall, the marker is saturated. It is not a metaphor: accumulated transitions are lost because the sites change again.

iSaturated does not mean useless

A saturated marker still resolves recent nodes well; what you cannot do is use it for the deep nodes and believe its support. The usual solution is not to throw the gene away: it is to remove the third codon position, switch to amino acids, or look for a slower marker for the deep part of the tree.

✦Why the genome is not decoration

The chloroplast and the mitochondrion of a plant are inherited from a single parent—in most angiosperms, from the mother—and do not recombine: the whole genome is a single lineage, with a single history. The nucleus is inherited from both and does recombine. That is why a chloroplast tree and a nuclear tree can disagree without either of them being wrong: chloroplast capture after an ancient hybridization leaves exactly that signature. Declaring the genome of each partition in Block 2 is what lets the following blocks treat that disagreement as information and not as noise.

2.1Why this block decides the study

Aligning two columns means claiming that they descend from the same ancestral site. It is not data preparation: it is the first hypothesis of the work, and it is the only one that no later block can put to the test. The maximum likelihood of Block 5 will take your alignment as a fact and compute on it in all seriousness; if the columns are wrong, it will compute a false answer in all seriousness.

✦The consequence you need to understand

A bad alignment does not produce a tree with little support, which would be the honest result. It produces a firmly wrong tree: the misaligned columns become false informative sites, and those sites enthusiastically support groupings that do not exist. The bootstrap will back them, because the bootstrap measures the consistency of the signal, and the signal is there—it is just artificial.

That is why this block does not stop at aligning: it brings a viewer to look at the result column by column, an editor to correct it by hand, a trimming step with a preview and a quality control that looks for eleven specific problems. The sentence at the head of the alignment section on screen says it plainly: the eye of someone who knows the group often beats the algorithm.

2.4Aligning: the aligner and its options

The aligner is progressive, from the school that Clustal made standard and MUSCLE refined. It works in four steps:

  1. Quick distances between each pair of sequences from the k-mers they share, without aligning anything yet.
  2. Guide tree by UPGMA on those distances.
  3. Profile-to-profile alignment traveling the guide tree from the leaves to the root, with affine gaps (Gotoh 1982) and with the penalty reduced where the profile already has gaps.
  4. Iterative refinement, optional: each branch of the guide tree splits the sequences in two, the two sub-alignments are realigned and the result is kept only if it improves the sum-of-pairs score. It is the third stage of MUSCLE.

There are four controls: by codon, refine, gap open and extend. The last two are Gotoh’s penalties: opening a gap costs a lot, extending it costs little, because an insertion of six bases is a single event and not six.

iHow good it is

Measured against the true alignment of the simulated examples, the aligner recovers 99.98% of the pairs in rbcL, 98.14% in trnL-F—the one that carries indels—, 98.93% in ITS and 99.60% in the protein family. Aligning rbcL by codon, 100%. Those figures are in the validation folder and can be reproduced.

2.5Aligning by codon

If the partition is coding, this is the most useful checkbox in the whole block. With by codon checked, the program translates, aligns as protein and returns the gaps to the nucleotides three at a time.

The consequence is that a gap of one or two bases cannot appear, and such a gap, in a coding region, is almost always an artifact of the aligner: it would imply a frameshift that selection would have removed. Aligning by codon does not improve the result a little: it eliminates an entire class of error at the root, and that is why the rbcL example goes from 99.98% to 100%.

2.8Trimming of ambiguous blocks

There are regions—highly variable spacers, poorly sequenced ends—where the alignment is simply not credible. Removing them reduces noise; removing too much erases exactly what is informative. The program offers two schools:

Gblocks (Castresana 2000)

It chooses by neighborhood rules: a column is kept if it and its neighbors meet certain requirements. Its parameters are conserved and highly conserved (how many sequences must agree), max contiguous (how many non-conserved columns in a row are tolerated), minimum block (the minimum length of a block that is kept) and gaps allowed, with three levels: none, up to half and all.

Per-column thresholds, in the style of trimAl

They decide column by column: by gap proportion, by column similarity, or gaps and similarity at once, with the max gaps and min similarity sliders.

Whatever the method, the program first shows what it is going to remove. The preview paints the doomed columns on the alignment, and nothing is touched until you click Apply the trimming; Clear the preview cancels. The keep whole codons checkbox forces whole codons to be removed, so as not to break the reading frame.

✕Always trimming, out of habit

Trimming does not always improve the tree. There are published studies where removing columns worsens the inference, because informative sites go out together with the noise. The honest check is to run the analysis with and without trimming and see whether the tree changes. If you lost more than half of the columns, the problem was not the noise: it was the alignment.

3.1What is decided in this block

The differences observed between two sequences underestimate the changes that really happened, because a site that changed from A to G and then to T is counted as one change, not two, and if it went back to A none is counted at all. A substitution model is the tool that turns what is observed into an estimate of what happened.

Choosing it well matters as much as choosing the inference method. A model that is too simple underestimates long branches—and thereby feeds the long-branch attraction of Chapter 1—; one that is too complex spends parameters the data cannot estimate and increases variance without removing bias.

✦Why “always use GTR+I+G” is not enough

It is what many people do, and with thousands of sites it rarely goes wrong. But with little data GTR+I+G has eight free parameters plus the branch lengths, and estimating them all on two hundred positions produces very imprecise estimates. Besides, +I and +G compete to explain the same thing: together they tend to give extreme values of both that do not mean what they seem to. This block exists so that the choice is made by fit and not by habit.

The block works on one aligned partition at a time, chosen in the partition menu. If you have several, section 4 of the panel also looks for how to group them.

3.2What your data contain

The first section summarizes the partition before touching anything: how many taxa, how many sites, how many columns are constant, how many variable and how many informative, the G+C content and the proportion of gaps.

The sentence at the head of the section says something worth understanding well: constant sites tell you nothing about the topology but plenty about the model. A column where all taxa have A does not choose between topologies, but it does say that there were no changes at that site, and that is direct information about the rates and about the proportion of invariable sites. That is why removing constant sites “because they contribute nothing” biases branch lengths upward: the model believes there was more change than there was.

3.3The assumptions, put to the test

Before choosing a model, the block checks the two assumptions that no model on the list relaxes.

Composition per sequence

All the usual models assume that base composition is the same in every sequence and throughout the tree. The plot shows the composition of each sequence, and a χ² test compares each one against the mean.

!What to do if composition is heterogeneous

What does not work is changing the substitution model: none of the 56 fixes it, because they all share that assumption. Two sequences with similar composition attract each other in the tree even if they are not related, and the support will be high. There are three real ways out: removing the third codon position, which is usually the culprit; switching to amino acids, whose composition varies less; or recoding the nucleotides into purines and pyrimidines. The help card of the χ² test says so on screen as well.

Saturation

The second plot is the one you practiced in Lab 2 of Chapter 1, now on your data: transitions and transversions against the corrected distance. The rule reads the same way: as long as the cloud follows a straight line, the marker is informative; when it bends and flattens, it is saturated. Transitions saturate before transversions, and the third codon position before the first and second.

Remember what saturated means and what it does not mean: the marker still resolves recent nodes well; what it cannot do is support the deep nodes and their support values.

3.5How the choice is made: AIC, AICc, BIC and weights

A model with more parameters always gives a better likelihood, even if the extra parameters are of no use at all. That is why the lnL is not compared on its own, but a quantity that rewards fit and penalizes parameters:

∑The three formulas

AIC = −2·lnL + 2k  ·  AICc = AIC + 2k(k+1)/(n−k−1)  ·  BIC = −2·lnL + k·ln(n)

where k is the number of parameters and n the number of sites. In all three, lower is better. AICc corrects for small samples and approaches AIC when n is large. BIC penalizes more heavily and therefore tends to choose simpler models.

What you read in the table is not the absolute value but the difference from the best, ΔAIC. Burnham and Anderson’s convention: below 2, the model is practically indistinguishable from the winner; between 4 and 10, clearly worse; above 10, discarded.

The Akaike weights column turns those differences into probabilities that sum to one: the weight of a model is the probability that it is the best of the set compared. A winner with a weight of 0.9 settles the matter; several models sharing the weight means that the data do not distinguish between them, and then it is worth checking that the tree does not change depending on which one is used.

!“The best of the set” is not “good”

If all the candidates are bad for these data—for example, because composition is heterogeneous and none of them models it—, the weight of 0.99 goes to the least bad. Choosing the model is not the analysis, and a GTR+Γ fitted on a crooked alignment is still a GTR+Γ fitted on a crooked alignment.

3.6Reading the winning model

Once the model is chosen, the program refits it properly, with all branches optimized, and shows its parts.

The rate matrix

The six exchangeability rates between pairs of bases and the four equilibrium frequencies. It is worth looking at the ratio between transitions and transversions: in nuclear and chloroplast DNA the normal value is around 2, and a value close to 1 in a gene that should have more is a sign of saturation—the transitions, which saturate first, have already been erased.

The shape of the gamma (α)

It describes how unequal the distribution of rates among sites is. Below 1 the heterogeneity is strong: a few very fast sites and many almost frozen. Above 3, rates are almost equal and +G adds little. The Rates among sites plot draws the discrete categories with their relative rate.

✕Interpreting α and the proportion of invariable sites separately in a +I+G

+I and +G explain the same phenomenon in two ways, and when they are fitted together they share the work unstably: it is common to get a high proportion of invariable sites and a low α, with neither value meaning what it seems to. Many authors recommend using +G alone. If you need to report I and α, do so stating that they come from a joint fit.

The verdict in words

Below the tables, the panel writes in prose which model won, by how much, and what its parameters mean. It is the text you can take almost as is into the methods section, and the one Block 12 reuses when it writes the report.

3.7Hierarchical likelihood ratio tests

The other way of choosing, older than the information criteria and still often requested by reviewers. It compares nested models—one is a special case of the other—with the statistic 2ΔlnL against a χ² with as many degrees of freedom as the difference in parameters, and chains the tests until it settles on the simplest model that is not rejected.

The program offers it because it is still asked for, and at the same time it says why information criteria are the preferred route today:

  • The result depends on the order in which the tests are chained, and there is no canonical order.
  • It only compares nested models, so it leaves out perfectly sensible comparisons.
  • The χ² is an asymptotic approximation and does not hold when a parameter sits on the boundary of its range, which is exactly the case of +I: the proportion of invariable sites is tested against zero, which is its limit.

3.8The partition scheme

With several partitions the question is not only which model, but how many different models are needed. Giving each gene its own is the most flexible and the most expensive in parameters; giving all of them a single one is the cheapest and may be false.

Find the scheme carries out a greedy merging in the style of PartitionFinder: it starts with one partition per gene, at each step tries the merger of two that improves the information criterion, and stops when no merger improves it. The result says which partitions share a model and which deserve their own.

With a gene split by codon position, the typical result is that the first and second positions merge and the third stays on its own, which is exactly what biology predicts.

The Export the chosen model button saves the scheme and the model to take them to another program or to keep a record.

3.9Frequent mistakes

✕Choosing the model without first looking at the assumptions

The two plots in section 2 of the panel are more important than the whole table of models. If composition is heterogeneous or the marker is saturated, the winning model will be the least bad of a list of unsuitable models, and its Akaike weight of 0.99 will say nothing about it.

✕Reporting only the name of the model

“GTR+I+G was used” is half the information. Missing are the criterion by which it was chosen, the number of gamma categories, and—if there were partitions—the scheme. The Block 12 report writes it out in full.

✕Removing constant sites

They tell you nothing about the topology, but they do about the model. Removing them inflates branch lengths. The only situation in which they are removed is when the model knows it and corrects for it, which is what Mkv does in morphology.

✕Believing that choosing the model well fixes the tree

It improves branch lengths, support and, in difficult cases, the topology. It does not fix a bad alignment, or a heterogeneous composition, or a taxon sampling that leaves a long branch on its own. The order of importance is: data, alignment, sampling, model.

4.1What quick trees are for

This block produces trees in seconds, and that makes them easy to look down on. It is not a good idea. They have three legitimate uses:

  • Starting point. The maximum likelihood search of Block 5 needs an initial tree, and the better it is, the less risk there is of getting stuck in a local optimum.
  • Contrast. If the distance tree, the parsimony tree and the likelihood tree agree on the main clades, that is information. If they disagree, so is that, and you need to understand why before publishing any of them.
  • The right tool for morphology. With discrete characters and few changes, parsimony is not a cheap approximation to likelihood: it is the method the discipline uses, and with good reason.
✦A distance tree is not a weak hypothesis

It is when the distances are poorly corrected, which is a different matter. With the right model, neighbor joining is a consistent estimator: with enough data it converges to the true tree. What ape calls logdet is the distance of Lockhart et al. (1994); the formula with the observed frequencies is Lake’s paralinear distance. They are often confused because they share the nickname. Here they appear separately and under their own names, so that you can report which one was used.

Two checkboxes modify the calculation. gamma correction applies the gamma correction with the α estimated by Block 3, and complete deletion decides what to do with gaps: when checked, it removes every column that has any gap before computing any pair; when unchecked, each pair uses the columns that both have. With a lot of missing data, complete deletion can leave you with very few columns.

The methods

The method menu offers five options: BIONJ, the one selected by default; neighbor joining (NJ), the classic method of Saitou and Nei; minimum evolution; UPGMA; and WPGMA, the weighted variant of UPGMA, which, when joining two groups, averages their distances giving them equal weight, regardless of how many sequences each one has.

!UPGMA and WPGMA assume a molecular clock

And most data do not meet it. Both produce an ultrametric tree—all tips at the same height—, which is correct for a clustering dendrogram and false for a phylogeny with unequal rates. Use them to see structure, not to infer relationships. NJ and BIONJ do not assume a clock.

Balanced minimum evolution looks for the tree whose total length, computed with the balanced averages of Desper and Gascuel (2002) on Pauplin’s (2000) criterion, is the smallest. It is a true optimality criterion, with NNI and SPR search from several starts, and not a mere clustering algorithm.

4.3Maximum parsimony

The tree that needs the fewest changes to explain the matrix. Transparent, free of model assumptions… and vulnerable for that very reason, as you saw in Lab 1 of Chapter 1: without a model that corrects for multiple changes, parsimony interprets convergence as kinship.

The controls

  • characters: unordered (Fitch), where any state can go to any other in one step; ordered (Sankoff), where the cost depends on the pair of states and an order can be imposed—useful in morphology, when a character has graded states—; or transversions weighted, which penalizes transversions more than transitions.
  • tv:ts weight: how much more a transversion costs. With 2:1, a transversion is worth two steps. It is parsimony’s way of acknowledging that transitions are more frequent.
  • swapping: NNI, SPR or TBR (Section 4.4).
  • starts: how many different addition orders are tried. Several starts that reach the same tree are the best sign that the search was sufficient.
  • max trees: how many equally short trees are kept. Morphological matrices sometimes produce thousands.

4.4The search: NNI, SPR and TBR

The number of topologies grows faster than factorially: with twenty taxa there are more than 10²⁰ unrooted trees. They cannot all be evaluated, so the search starts from one and tries rearrangements.

Table 4.2. The three types of rearrangement, from smallest to largest reach.
TypeWhat it doesWhen
NNISwaps the two groups adjacent to an internal branch. Small moves.Fast; easily gets stuck in local optima.
SPRPulls off a branch with everything hanging from it and regrafts it elsewhere.The usual balance between reach and time.
TBRCuts the tree in two and rejoins the halves through every possible pair of branches.The most exhaustive and the slowest.

No heuristic search guarantees the optimum. When two programs give different trees under the same criterion, it is usually this and not a disagreement of method: they are two local optima.

4.5The indices: CI, RI, RC and HI

Once the tree is in place, four indices describe how much homoplasy—convergence and reversal—it contains.

∑The four definitions

CI = m/s, the minimum possible number of changes over the number the tree needs. It equals 1 without homoplasy.
HI = 1 − CI, the homoplasy index: the same thing said the other way round.
RI = (g−s)/(g−m), where g is the length on the worst possible tree: how much of the similarity that could be synapomorphy actually is.
RC = CI × RI, the rescaled consistency index.

!The CI falls with the number of taxa

Systematically, and without homoplasy having increased. A CI of 0.8 with fifteen taxa and one of 0.4 with two hundred may describe the same matrix. That is why CIs are not compared between studies of different size, and why the RI, which corrects part of the problem, is the one worth reporting.

4.6Branch support

The sentence at the head of this section on screen deserves repeating: a clade without support is not a bug: it is a polytomy your data cannot resolve. The program offers three measures.

Bootstrap

The alignment columns are resampled with replacement, the tree is rebuilt hundreds of times and the percentage of replicates in which each branch appears is counted. It measures the consistency of the signal in the data, not the probability that the branch is correct. The convention: 70% for “well supported,” 95% for strong.

Jackknife

The same, but instead of resampling with replacement a fraction of the columns is deleted in each replicate. It gives results similar to the bootstrap and is somewhat more conservative.

Bremer support

Different in nature: how many extra steps have to be accepted before finding a tree in which that branch no longer appears. It is read in steps, not as a percentage, and it depends on the size of the matrix: in a large matrix, a Bremer of 5 may be little. A value of 1 means that one step is enough to undo the branch.

✕Believing that the bootstrap protects against systematic error

It does not, and it is important to understand this. The bootstrap measures whether the signal holds up when the same data are resampled under the same method. If the method is biased—long-branch attraction, heterogeneous composition—, the wrong signal is in all the replicates, and the bootstrap will support the wrong branch at 100%. With more data, it will support it more.

4.7Consensus and comparison

When the search returns several equally short trees, the question is what they agree on. The menu offers four consensus methods:

The drawing uses the same engine that the tree studio of Block 12 will use: rectangular, circular or unrooted layout; cladogram to draw without lengths; support as numbers, colored dots or none; and root as it is, midpoint or outgroup…, with multiple selection using Ctrl.

iAn unrooted tree has no ancestors

4.9How well it does, measured

Everything in this block was checked against ape and phangorn, and the results are in the validation folder. Three deserve mention:

  • The parsimony search reaches 1,916 steps on the example, the same optimum as phangorn’s pratchet, and thirteen steps better than optim.parsimony with SPR.
  • The balanced minimum evolution length reproduces that of ape::fastme.bal to within 5·10⁻⁹ on the same tree, so the formula agrees exactly.
  • With its own search from three starts, the program reaches a shorter tree (2.899076) than ape’s (2.901969). It is not that one of them is wrong: they are two local optima of the same criterion, and where a heuristic lands depends on where it starts.

4.10Frequent mistakes

✕Using uncorrected distances

It is what gave distance methods their bad reputation. With saturation, the p distance underestimates divergence exactly where it matters most, and the tree gets distorted. Always correct, and with the model that Block 3 chose.

✕Publishing a UPGMA tree as a phylogeny

UPGMA assumes a molecular clock. If your rates are unequal—and they almost always are—, the tree will be wrong in a systematic and predictable way: the fast lineages will come out more “derived” than they are.

✕Reporting a single shortest tree without saying how many there were

If the search found 4,800 equally short trees and you publish one, you are presenting as a result an arbitrary choice among 4,800. What is reported is the consensus, the number of optimal trees and their length.

✕Comparing the CI with that of another study

It is only comparable between matrices of the same size. Report the RI, and in any case say how many taxa and how many characters the matrix had.

✕Accepting the search with a single start

A single starting point and NNI is the recipe for getting stuck in a local optimum. Raise the starts, use SPR or TBR, and check that several starts reach the same length. If they do not, the search is not over.

5.1What it optimizes, and why it matters

The likelihood of a tree is the probability of having observed these data if that tree and that model were true. The maximum-likelihood tree is the one that makes that probability highest. It is reported as a logarithm, always negative, and less negative is better.

!A lnL on its own means nothing

Its absolute value depends on the number of sites and taxa. It only makes sense to compare likelihoods computed on exactly the same data. A lnL of −18,674 is neither “bad” nor “good”: it is the one for these data under this model, and it is only useful against another from the same alignment.

The decisive advantage over parsimony is the one from Chapter 1: under the correct model, likelihood is consistent, that is, with enough data it converges to the true tree even when there are unequal long branches. Parsimony, in that same geometry, converges with growing certainty to the wrong tree.

The condition “under the correct model” is not rhetoric. If the model is misspecified—unmodeled compositional heterogeneity, uncorrected saturation—likelihood is wrong too, and with more data it is wrong with more confidence. That is why Block 3 comes before this one.

5.2The model and the starting tree

The first row of controls sets the model: model (GTR, HKY, LG…), +G with so many categories, +I, and the frequencies, which can be empirical—counted in the alignment—or equal. The Use the model chosen in Block 3 button copies what won there, which is almost always the right thing to do.

iThe estimate them (+F) checkbox

By default the base frequencies are counted from the alignment, which is what phangorn::optim.pml does without optBf, and that is why the program’s results match its own. Turning on +F estimates them by maximum likelihood together with everything else, which usually gives a slightly better lnL. It is off by default to preserve that comparability, not because it is worse.

The starting tree can be the parsimony (Block 4) tree, the distance (Block 4) tree, BIONJ, or a random one. This is no minor detail: the search is heuristic, and where it starts determines which local optimum it may get stuck in. Trying two different starting points and reaching the same lnL is the best evidence that the search was enough.

5.3The search: NNI, SPR and the radius

The search starts from the starting tree and tries rearrangements: first NNI, which swaps neighboring branches, and then SPR, which cuts a whole branch off and grafts it back somewhere else. The rearrangements menu lets you use NNI only or NNI + SPR.

Two controls govern the cost:

  • SPR radius: the maximum distance, in number of branches, at which regrafting is allowed. A small radius is fast and explores little; a large one explores a lot and takes long. Five is a reasonable balance.
  • max rounds: how many complete passes of rearrangements are made before stopping.
∑Why the search is fast without cheating

Scoring each candidate by reoptimizing the whole tree would be unfeasible. The program does what PhyML and RAxML do: for each move, it reoptimizes only the branches the move touched, relying on the incremental computation of the likelihood engine. SPR also runs in two passes: first all candidates are scored with the inherited branch lengths—RAxML’s “lazy SPR”—and then only the best dozen are scored again with their neighborhood reoptimized. The winning tree is refitted in full at the end.

5.4Reading the result

The tiles summarize the run: the log-likelihood, the number of parameters (branches plus model), AIC and BIC, how many rounds were needed and how many topologies were evaluated, the time, and the estimated model parameters—the gamma α and κ or the relative rates.

Below the tiles, the panel writes out in prose what they mean. Two things deserve attention:

  • The number of rounds. If the search ended in a single round, it may not have explored anything; if it used up the maximum, it was probably still improving and you should raise it.
  • The α. Below 1 there is strong heterogeneity among sites, and not modeling it would have distorted the long branches.

5.5One model per partition

A chloroplast gene and nuclear ITS do not evolve at the same rate nor with the same composition. The partitioned fit acknowledges this without breaking the tree: all partitions share the topology and the branch proportions, but each has its own model and a rate multiplier, normalized to a site-weighted mean of one.

So a partition with a multiplier of 0.3 evolves at a third of the mean rate, and one with 2.5, two and a half times faster.

iWhy the fit starts out “tied”

Releasing all partitions from the very start made the search fall into a worse optimum. The program starts with all of them sharing one model during the first passes and releases them afterward. It is an implementation detail, but it explains why the partitioned result never falls below the single model it contains.

!Partitioning does not resolve a real conflict

Giving each gene its own rate does not free it from sharing the tree. If two genes have genuinely different histories—chloroplast capture, hybridization—the partitioned analysis averages them more elegantly, but it averages them all the same. Uncovering them is the job of Block 11.

5.6The four support measures

This is the section of the chapter that prevents the most mistakes. The program computes four measures, and they do not mean the same thing nor are they read against the same threshold.

Table 5.1. The four support measures, with their conventional threshold.
MeasureThresholdWhat it measures
Classical bootstrap≥ 70In what percentage of alignments resampled with replacement the branch reappears. Conservative: 70 is already fair support.
Ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot)≥ 95An approximation by RELL, built to be nearly unbiased. Its scale is not that of the classical bootstrap.
SH-aLRT≥ 80Compares the likelihood of the tree with that of the two NNI rearrangements of that branch. A different question, not another version of the same one.
aBayes—A fast Bayesian approximation on the same local comparison.
✕The most common mistake with UFBoot

Reading it with the classical bootstrap threshold. Because UFBoot is nearly unbiased, it is less conservative: a UFBoot of 90% is not equivalent to a bootstrap of 90% but to considerably less support. That is why its threshold is 95 and not 70. The header of the on-screen table has the threshold written in, precisely so they are not confused.

Current practice in the literature is to require both: SH-aLRT ≥ 80 and UFBoot ≥ 95. They are different information—one measures whether the branch improves the likelihood, the other whether the signal holds up under resampling—and a branch that passes both is much better supported than one that passes only one.

✦What none of the four measures

Whether the tree is correct. All four measure the internal consistency of the signal under the method and model used. If the model is wrong, all four will support the wrong branch, and with more data they will support it more. High support is necessary, not sufficient.

5.7Testing a hypothesis

Here the block stops describing and starts testing. The question is: do my data reject the tree proposed by another author, or the one my hypothesis proposes?

It is done in two steps:

  1. Select the taxa of the group being tested and press Search for the best tree under that constraint: the program searches for the best tree forcing that group to be monophyletic. That is the best possible case for the hypothesis.
  2. Press Compare the trees (KH, SH, AU): the three tests contrast the constrained tree against the unconstrained one, using RELL replicates.

A p-value below 0.05 rejects the alternative tree. Of the three, the AU test is the one that best corrects for selection bias and the one you should report; KH and SH are there because they are still requested.

!“Not rejected” is not “just as good”

With few data almost nothing is rejected, and with many almost everything is. A nonsignificant result says that your data are not enough to rule out that hypothesis, not that it is correct. And the set of trees to compare must be fixed before looking at the results: choosing afterward what to compare is exactly the bias the AU test tries to correct.

5.8Threads, and why it takes as long as it does

The bootstrap and the Bayesian chains are spread over browser threads, built from the program’s own code. Two measured facts are worth knowing:

  • A freshly created thread runs interpreted and only reaches optimized code after a few seconds of repeating the same loops. That is why 16 replicates on 16 threads take longer than the same 16 on six: each thread pays its whole warm-up and throws it away. The program never assigns fewer than three jobs per thread.
  • One hundred maximum-likelihood bootstrap replicates take minutes. This is JavaScript, not C, and the page says so before starting, with an estimate and a cancel button.

If the browser does not allow threads—some configurations block them—the work is done in the page, slower but without failing.

5.10Common mistakes

✕Reading UFBoot with the bootstrap threshold

Already said, and repeated because it is the most common: 70 for the classical bootstrap, 95 for the ultrafast one, 80 for SH-aLRT. Three measures, three thresholds.

✕Searching only once, from a single starting tree

The search is heuristic and does not guarantee the optimum. If two different starting points reach different lnL values, the higher one rules and the search had not finished. Reaching the same one is the only reasonable evidence that it had.

✕Comparing lnL across different alignments

Removing three columns changes the lnL, and not because the tree is worse. Likelihoods are only compared on exactly the same data: same alignment, same columns, same taxa.

✕Presenting a constrained tree as “the tree”

The constrained tree is the best case for the hypothesis being tested, not an estimate. It is computed in order to compare it, and what gets published is the comparison—the lnL of both and the p-value of the AU test—not the constrained tree by itself.

✕Believing that high support validates the model

It is the other way around: support is computed assuming the model. If Block 3 warned of compositional heterogeneity or saturation, a UFBoot of 100 does not refute it; it repeats it with more confidence.

6.1What changes with respect to likelihood

Maximum likelihood asks: which tree makes these data most probable? Bayesian inference asks the opposite: how probable is each tree, given these data? To answer that, something more is needed: a prior distribution stating what was believed before looking at the data.

The result is not a tree but a distribution over trees, branch lengths and parameters. From it come the posterior probabilities of each clade and the credible intervals of each age, which is what makes this block the natural companion of Block 7.

✦The prior is not a weakness of the method: it is an obligation to write it down

Those who criticize the Bayesian approach for “depending on assumptions” tend to forget that likelihood also makes assumptions—the model, independence among sites, compositional homogeneity—it just does not force you to declare them. Here you have to write them down, and that is an advantage: the assumptions are in plain sight and can be discussed.

6.2What is assumed before seeing the data

The first section of the panel is the list of priors. The Use the model from Block 5 button copies the model already chosen; the next three fields set the priors:

  • mean branch length (exponential): the mean of the exponential on each branch length.
  • mean of α (exponential): the prior on the shape of the gamma.
  • mean of the rates (exponential): the prior on the rates of the substitution model.

In addition, the program uses a uniform prior on topologies—all equally probable before seeing the data—and a Dirichlet on the base frequencies.

!The branch-length prior is not harmless

It is the assumption that most quietly bends a result. With the exponential of mean 0.1—MrBayes’s default—and divergent data, the posterior of the total tree length comes out at half that of the maximum-likelihood tree: the prior pulls the branches down and wins. This is documented (Brown et al. 2010; Marshall 2010), and the panel warns about it before running, comparing the prior with the length that Block 5 measured.

The solution is not to remove the prior—that cannot be done—but to choose it deliberately and check that the result does not change with another reasonable one.

6.3The chain and its coupling

The sampler is Metropolis–Hastings over topologies, branch lengths and model parameters. At each step it proposes a change and accepts or rejects it according to a rule that guarantees that, in the long run, the frequency with which it visits each tree is that tree’s posterior probability.

There are six controls:

Table 6.1. The chain controls.
ControlWhat it is
generationsHow many steps each chain takes. More is better and slower.
burn-inThe first generations, while the chain climbs from where it was released, are thrown away.
sample everyHow many steps between saved samples. Saving all of them adds no information, because they are correlated.
runsHow many independent chains, started separately. With fewer than two, convergence cannot be checked.
chains per runThe coupled chains (MC³) of each run.
heatingHow much the posterior of the heated chains is “flattened.”
∑What coupling (MC³) is for

A single chain can get trapped on one peak of the posterior and never leave, because to reach another peak it would have to cross a valley of low probability. Coupling runs several chains at once: one cold chain, which is the one that samples, and several heated ones that see a flattened posterior and move much more freely. Every so often, two chains swap positions. This way the cold chain can jump to a peak the heated one found, without ceasing to sample the correct distribution.

Each run executes in its own browser thread, so four runs make use of four cores.

6.4Did it converge? The four numbers

The program puts the diagnostics before the tree, and not on a whim: a Bayesian tree from a chain that did not converge is a pretty picture of nothing.

Table 6.2. The four convergence numbers and their threshold.
NumberTargetWhat it says
ESS≥ 200How many independent samples the ones the chain took are worth. Ten thousand correlated samples may be worth thirty.

✕Trusting a flat trace

A flat trace does not prove convergence: it may be a stuck chain that has spent a million generations without leaving a local optimum. That is why you need several independent runs and the PSRF, which compares among them. And the PSRF needs the runs to start at different places: if all of them start from the same tree, they can give 1.00 and all be equally wrong.

A high ESS is not enough on its own either: it says that the chain mixed well where it was, not that it covered the whole space.

6.5Posterior probabilities

The tree that is reported is the majority-rule consensus of the samples, with the frequency of each clade as its posterior probability. The conventional support threshold is 0.95.

!A posterior is not a bootstrap

They are different scales and are not compared. For the same branch, the posterior probability is usually considerably higher than the bootstrap: it is not that the Bayesian method “supports more,” it is that it answers another question. Moreover, the posterior is known to be overconfident when the model is misspecified: it can give 1.00 to a branch that the bootstrap supports at 60%. A 1.00 is not a guarantee; it is a statement conditional on the model and the priors.

The panel also shows the credible set of trees: how many distinct trees are needed to accumulate 95% of the posterior. If three are needed, the result is firm; if eight hundred are needed, the topology is far from resolved, however well supported the consensus may look on some branches.

6.6Bayes factors

AIC and BIC compare models with a shortcut: they penalize the number of parameters. The Bayes factor compares them by integrating over all possible values of those parameters according to the prior, which is more costly and more correct.

The quantity being integrated is the marginal likelihood, and the program estimates it by stepping stones (Xie et al. 2011): a series of intermediate distributions between the prior and the posterior, with the steps and generations per step controls. Never by the harmonic mean, which is notoriously bad.

Table 6.3. The Kass and Raftery scale on 2·ln(BF).
2·ln(BF)Evidence in favor of the better model

✕Comparing Bayes factors computed with different priors

The marginal likelihood is much more sensitive to the prior than the posterior, because it integrates over it. Two people with the same model and different priors can obtain opposite Bayes factors. And the estimate has its own error: repeating it with another seed tells you how much, and it is worth doing before publishing a 2·ln(BF) of 7.

6.7Taking the result away

The block exports the consensus tree with its posterior probabilities and a Tracer-compatible log, so the diagnostics can be reviewed with the tool the community uses. The tree passes on to Block 7 just like the maximum-likelihood one, and for dating it is often preferable, because the Bayesian relaxed clock gives a distribution per node instead of a point.

6.8How the sampler was checked to be correct

An MCMC sampler is hard to validate: there is no reference number to compare against, because the result is a distribution. This block was checked in three different ways, and all three are in the validation folder.

1 · Switching off the likelihood

If the likelihood is disconnected, the sampler must reproduce its own prior exactly, because there are no longer any data to pull it away. It is the most severe test it can be given, and it passes. In the recorded run—400,000 generations; the bench is stochastic, so another run gives different last decimals—:

  • The three unrooted topologies of four taxa come out at 0.3374, 0.3335 and 0.3292, against an expected 1/3.
  • With six taxa, all 105 possible topologies are visited, with a mean frequency of 0.00952, against 1/105 = 0.00952.
  • Branch lengths give a mean of 0.10060 against the exponential of mean 0.1, and the total tree length 0.50302 against the 0.5 of the corresponding Gamma(5, 0.1).

2 · Comparing the diagnostics with coda

The HPD and PSRF are computed on a series that R and the browser build in the same way, and they come out identical to those of coda, which is the reference implementation.

3 · Auditing the chain move by move

An audit compares the likelihood the chain keeps on record—computed incrementally, for speed—with a computation from scratch, at every move. The mismatch is zero in all of them.

iA finding only the sampler could uncover

During those tests the chain gave likelihoods thirty units above the supposed maximum of Block 5. It was not a bug: the chain estimates the base frequencies, while the maximum-likelihood fit kept them fixed at the empirical ones. That is where the +F checkbox of Block 5 came from, which now lets you estimate them there too.

6.9Common mistakes

✕Looking at the tree before the diagnostics

The panel puts them in that order on purpose. With an ESS below 200, a PSRF above 1.01 or an ASDSF above 0.01, you do not look at the tree: you lengthen the run.

✕Running a single chain

Without at least two independent runs there is no PSRF or ASDSF, and therefore no way of knowing whether it converged. A single chain with a beautiful trace may have been stuck for a million generations.

✕Reporting a posterior as if it were a bootstrap

“95% support” without saying which measure it is invites the reader to read it on the wrong scale. Write “posterior probability of 0.95,” and if there is also a bootstrap, give the two separately.

✕Leaving the default branch-length prior without looking at it

With divergent data, the exponential of mean 0.1 can halve the tree length. The panel compares the prior with what Block 5 measured and warns before running: that warning must be read.

✕Discarding 25% out of habit

Discarding a quarter of the generations is the convention, but looking at the trace is better than the convention. Sometimes burn-in ends at 2% and good data are being thrown away; sometimes it has not ended at 50%.

7.1What the clock cannot give you on its own

The branches of the tree produced by Blocks 5 and 6 are measured in substitutions per site. Not in years. A branch of 0.08 can be a million years of fast evolution or fifty of slow evolution, and the sequences, by themselves, cannot tell those two cases apart.

✦The confounding of rate and time

What a branch measures is rate × time. With one observed number and two unknowns, outside information is needed to separate them. That is the whole difficulty of molecular dating, and it explains why a date depends more on its calibrations than on any statistical refinement.

That is why the block asks for three things in this order: a root, because without it there is no direction and no ages; an answer to does the clock hold?, which decides which method to use; and at least one calibration, without which ages come out as proportions of the depth of the tree and not in years.

7.2The root

A tree without a root has no direction and therefore no ages. The root by menu offers four routes:

Table 7.1. The four ways of placing the root.

outgroupThe honest way when you have one. It is a hypothesis, and it can be wrong: an outgroup that is too distant introduces a long branch and can attract the most divergent taxon of the ingroup.
midpointPlaces the root at the center of the longest path. Convenient, but it assumes a clock—exactly what is about to be tested. Use it to explore, not to publish.

leave it as it isIf the tree already came rooted from another block or from a file.

The root-to-tip regression

With dates at the tips, the program plots the distance from the root to each tip against its date. If there is a clock, the two rise together in a straight line and the slope is the rate. The r² tells how much of the variation time explains.

!The r² of this regression is optimistic

The root-to-tip distances of two sister taxa are not independent: they share almost the whole path. That is why the r² comes out higher than it should and must not be used as a formal test of the clock. It is useful for looking, for spotting odd tips and for estimating an approximate rate.

7.3Does the clock hold?

The test compares two fits of the same tree: one with free branches, one length per branch, and another forced to be ultrametric, where all tips sit at the same height. The difference in log-likelihood, multiplied by two, is tested against a χ² with n−2 degrees of freedom.

A small p-value rejects the strict clock and calls for a relaxed clock.

✕Reading “not rejected” as “there is a clock”

It does not prove it. The test has little power with few taxa: with twelve species it rejects almost nothing. Conversely, with many sites it almost always rejects, because it detects tiny deviations of no practical importance. The panel says so in those words when the result is not significant.

7.4The calibrations

Without at least one, ages do not exist: substitutions per site and millions of years are two different scales and nothing links them. The program accepts four sources of years and, in the type menu, five prior shapes.

Table 7.2. The five prior shapes and what they assert.
ShapeWhat it says about the node
fixed ageIt has exactly this age, with no uncertainty. Almost never true in biology.
uniform intervalIt lies between these two ages, with no preferred point. The shape of a geological event.
normalIt lies around a mean, with a deviation (mean / M and sd / S), equally likely toward younger and older ages. The shape of an age taken from another study; never that of a fossil, because it would allow the node to be younger than the fossil.
offset lognormalIt is no younger than the offset, with a long tail backward. The shape of a fossil.
offset exponentialThe same, with the mass more concentrated near the offset.
!A fossil gives the minimum age, not the age

This is the idea to take away from this chapter. When that individual died, the lineage already existed, and it may have existed long before: what the fossil proves is that the node is no younger than it. That is why asymmetric, offset priors are used, which allow the node to be much older but not younger. Entering the fossil’s age as a fixed age asserts that the lineage arose exactly when that individual died, and squeezes the whole tree toward the present.

To select the node you choose the taxa of the clade—none means the root. If those taxa do not form a clade in the tree, the program says so instead of calibrating something other than what was asked.

And it warns about the mistakes that ruin a dating

  • A fossil entered as a fixed age, or with a symmetric normal prior, which would allow a node younger than the fossil.
  • An offset of zero, which imposes no minimum age: it allows the node to be from yesterday.
  • A normal whose lower tail reaches into the future, that is, into negative ages.
  • A prior so wide it barely informs, or so narrow it feigns a certainty nobody has.
  • The confusion of crown and stem: the taxa you select calibrate the crown node—the most recent common ancestor of the group. If your fossil is sister to the group and not a member of it, what it calibrates is the stem, which is older, and for that you also have to select the sister group.
  • That this is going to be the only calibration, and therefore that the whole scale of the tree depends on it.
iWhy there is no fossil database

And there will not be one. Assigning a fossil to a node is a taxonomic judgment about that fossil’s characters and about which clade it is sister to: it is the part that is debated in papers and the part a reviewer examines. A lookup table would invite publishing a date that nobody looked at. The program helps with everything surrounding that judgment; the judgment is yours.

7.6The three dating methods

Three routes, from the fastest to the most honest about uncertainty.

Least squares

Fits the ages by minimizing a least-squares function on the branch lengths (To et al. 2016). It gives a point estimate in seconds, even with five hundred taxa. It is what you use to see the order of magnitude, or when the tree is too large for the others.

Penalized likelihood

Lets the rate change between branches but penalizes abrupt changes (Sanderson 2002). The smoothing λ parameter controls how much: a large λ approaches a strict clock, a small λ lets each branch have its own rate.

!λ is not chosen by eye

It is chosen by cross-validation, removing one branch at a time and seeing which λ predicts best. The choose it by cross-validation button does exactly that and plots the curve. If the minimum of that curve falls at one end of the range tried, the range was too narrow and has to be widened.

Bayesian relaxed clock

An uncorrelated lognormal clock (Drummond et al. 2006): each branch draws its rate from a lognormal, independently of its neighbor. It is the slowest and the only one that returns a distribution per node instead of a point, which is what should be published when calibrations are uncertain—that is, almost always.

7.7Reading the chronogram

The tiles summarize: the root age, the method, the computing time, the mean rate in substitutions per site per unit of time, and the rate variation, a coefficient of variation that says how far the tree departs from a strict clock: near 0, there practically was a clock; above 0.5, rates are very unequal and a strict clock would have given wrong dates.

The chronogram is drawn on the geological time scale—the bands of the International Chronostratigraphic Chart—with uncertainty bars at each node.

✕Publishing an age without its interval

A node dated at 30 Ma with an interval of 28 to 32 is well determined; the same 30 Ma with an interval of 8 to 70 says almost nothing, even though the mean looks just as round. An age without an interval is not a result. And the interval only captures the uncertainty within the model and the calibrations you gave: it does not include the error of having chosen a calibration badly, which is usually the largest of all.

Without any calibration, the panel fixes the root at 1 and warns that the ages are proportions of the depth of the tree, not years: they serve to compare nodes with each other, not to write “X million years ago.”

7.9Common mistakes

✕Using the midpoint and then testing the clock

Midpoint rooting assumes a clock. Testing the clock on a tree rooted that way is asking the method what the method already took for granted. With an outgroup, the test is legitimate.

✕A single calibration

The whole scale of the tree hangs on it, and its error propagates to every node with nothing to offset it. Two or three well-spread calibrations are much better than one, however good, because they also check each other.

✕Calibrating with a secondary age without widening its uncertainty

An age taken from another study carries the tree, the model and the calibrations of that work. Copying its interval as it is pretends that uncertainty does not exist. The assistant proposes a 12% deviation and recommends widening it, not reducing it.

✕Confusing crown age with stem age

The crown is the most recent common ancestor of the living species of the group; the stem, its divergence from the sister group, and it is always older. The diversification rates of Block 8 are computed with one or the other and give different results. You have to say which one was used.

✕Reporting another study’s rate as if it were data

A rate published for “the same marker” was estimated in another group, with other sampling and other calibrations. It is the largest source of error in a dating and the least reported.

A chronogram does not only say when: the way its branching times are distributed says how fast the group diversified, and whether that pace changed. 8.1What is read in the branching times

This whole block is read off the branching times of the chronogram, so you need to have gone through Block 7. Without dates there is nothing to analyze: lengths in substitutions do not say at what pace diversity appeared.

The question being answered is a single one, in many forms: did the group always diversify at the same pace, or was there an early burst that slowed down, or a recent acceleration? And behind it, the uncomfortable one: how much extinction was there, and can it be known?

!The tree being analyzed is the survivors’ tree

A chronogram of living species is a reconstructed tree: the lineages that went extinct left no tips and are not there. Almost all the difficulty of the block comes from that amputation, and it explains why net diversification is estimated well and extinction, badly.

8.2Lineages through time and the γ statistic

The lineages-through-time curve counts how many lineages existed at each moment in the past. Drawn on a logarithmic scale, a constant rate gives a straight line: that is the reference against which everything else is judged.

  • If the curve bends down near the present, the nodes piled up early: the group diversified early and then slowed down.
  • If it bends up, the opposite happened.

Pybus and Harvey’s (2000) γ statistic puts a number on that. It compares where the nodes fall with where they would fall under a constant rate. Under a constant rate γ follows a standard normal distribution, so that:

∑How γ is read

γ < −1.645 rejects a constant rate in favor of a slowdown, one-tailed, at 5%.
Between −1.645 and 1.645, compatible with a constant rate.
Above 1.645, acceleration toward the present.

If there are several trees—from a bootstrap or from a posterior—the panel also draws a band over the sample, which is much more informative than the curve of a single tree.

8.3The incomplete-sampling trap

This is the section that saves papers.

✦Missing species imitate a slowdown exactly

When the whole group is not sampled, the missing species come mostly from the recent nodes, because young clades have few species and losing one is enough to erase a node. The result is a curve that flattens near the present—indistinguishable from a real slowdown. With 60% sampling, γ will come out negative even if the rate was perfectly constant.

That is why the MCCR test (Monte Carlo constant rates) exists. Instead of comparing γ against the standard normal, it simulates constant-rate trees with the same incomplete sampling as the data, measures their γ, and compares the observed γ against that distribution. The panel asks for:

  • species in the clade (in total, not those in the tree): the real number of described species, which is the figure you have to look up in the literature.
  • replicates: how many trees are simulated to build the null distribution.

The result is a critical value at 5% that will be more negative than −1.645, and how much more negative it is says how much slowdown the sampling explained on its own. If your observed γ does not cross that critical value, there is no slowdown to report.

✕Giving the wrong total number of species

The whole test depends on that figure. If you enter the species in the tree instead of those in the group, the test undercorrects and you will “find” the false slowdown again. If you exaggerate the total, it overcorrects and you will lose a real slowdown.

!The two-rate model estimates where the shift is

And that is why it has an unfair advantage: it will always find the best possible place to put the jump, even in data generated with a constant rate. AIC penalizes the extra parameter, but not the fact of having searched for it. A high weight for this model is a clue, not a proof.

8.5λ, μ, r and ε: what can be believed

The birth–death model estimates four related quantities:

Table 8.2. The four parameters, and how far they can be believed.
SymbolWhat it isReliability

✦An ε estimated at zero does not mean there was no extinction

It means that the survivors’ tree cannot see it. It is the same problem as the e of biogeography in Chapter 10: the lineages that disappeared left no trace in the tips. With fossils, the estimate changes completely. Reporting r with confidence and ε with reservations is the honest thing to do.

The panel also gives the doubling time, ln(2)/r: how long the group takes to double its number of lineages at the estimated rate. It is the same information as r, stated in units you can picture—and compared with the age of the group, it tells you at once whether the rate explains the current diversity.

8.6When there is only an age and a richness

Sometimes there is no tree of the group: only its age and how many species it has are known. The estimators of Magallón and Sanderson (2001) make do with that, and they are the ones used to compare hundreds of clades at once.

The panel asks for the number of species, the age, and—crucially—whether the age is of the crown or the stem.

✕Confusing crown with stem

The crown is the most recent common ancestor of the living species of the group; the stem is its divergence from the sister group, and it is always older. With the stem, the same richness is spread over more time and the rate comes out lower. The two estimators are different and the program explicitly asks which one it is. A “group age” without a qualifier, taken from another paper, cannot be reused.

The estimator also needs an assumed value of ε, precisely because it cannot be estimated: the custom is to report the result with ε = 0 and with ε = 0.9, to show how much it depends on the assumption.

8.7A rate for every species: DR

The previous models give one rate for the whole group. The DR statistic (Jetz et al. 2012) hands out a rate among the tips: for each species it weights the branches on the path to the root, giving more weight to the recent ones.

It is read in relative terms: which tips have high DR compared with the others in the same tree. The absolute values depend on the scale of the tree and are not compared across studies.

!DR is not a per-species diversification rate

Even though it is very often used as one. It has been shown to estimate speciation better than net diversification, and to behave poorly when extinction is high (Title & Rabosky 2019). As a descriptive measure for ranking tips it is useful; as an estimator of r, it is not.

With the present drawn properly, γ gives a mean of −0.066 and a standard deviation of 0.9999, against 0.073 and 0.952 from ape::rphylo.

8.9Common mistakes

✕Reporting a negative γ without the MCCR test

It is the most common mistake of the block and the easiest to avoid. If you did not sample 100% of the group, your negative γ may be entirely an artifact of sampling. The MCCR test is two clicks away.

✕Interpreting μ or ε as biological quantities

From a tree of living species, the extinction rate is not estimated: it is assumed. Report r, say which ε you assumed, and show how the result changes with another.

✕Comparing DR across studies

It depends on the scale of the tree and on the sampling. It serves to rank the tips of your tree, not to say that your genus diversifies faster than your neighbor’s.

✕Analyzing a chronogram whose dates do not hold up

This whole block inherits the uncertainty of Block 7. If the ages have intervals as wide as the tree, γ and the five models are describing noise. Before interpreting a slowdown, look at the intervals of the ages that produce it.

✕Telling a slowdown as an “adaptive radiation”

A curve that flattens is compatible with niche saturation, but also with incomplete sampling, with recent extinction and with a badly calibrated chronogram. The pattern does not identify the process, and the block measures the pattern.

9.1Why species are not independent data

Suppose you measure seed size in forty species and relate it to plant height. An ordinary regression treats those forty species as forty independent observations. They are not: if thirty belong to the same genus and inherited both the large seed and the height from their common ancestor, what you have is not thirty data points but, at bottom, one repeated thirty times.

✦The practical consequence

Ignoring relatedness inflates significance: p-values come out much smaller than they should, and relationships get published that do not hold up. The methods in this block are not a statistical refinement: they are the correction of a counting error.

You work on a tree, preferably the dated one from Block 7, because the models speak of rates per unit of time.

9.2The character table

One row per species and one column per character, separated by commas, semicolons or tabs. The program infers the type of each column—continuous or discrete—, recognizes missing data and matches the names with the tree, forgiving the difference between Bursera_palmeri and Bursera palmeri, which is the one that wastes the most time.

Whatever does not match is listed, not silently swallowed. And each analysis prunes the tree to the tips that have the character it needs, and says how many it kept.

9.3Three models for a continuous character

Table 9.1. The three models, in the parameterization of geiger.
ModelWhat process it describes
Brownian motion (BM)Pure drift: the character wanders at random, and the variance between two species grows with the time since they split. A single parameter, σ².

They are compared by AIC with Akaike weights, and the panel writes the verdict in words.

The half-life, instead of α

OU’s α is hard to interpret by eye. The program reports the half-life, ln(2)/α: the time the character takes to travel half the way toward its optimum. And it is read by comparing it with the depth of the tree:

  • Half-life much shorter than the depth: strong stabilizing selection, and the tree hardly matters for predicting a species’ value.
  • Half-life longer than the depth: OU is indistinguishable from Brownian motion, and its α means nothing.
!Two warnings about OU

First: OU and early burst assume contemporaneous tips. On a non-ultrametric phylogram their parameters do not mean what they say, and the block measures the spread of the tip heights and warns you.

Second: with fewer than fifty species OU is often preferred for statistical, not biological reasons. It is a model very good at fitting noise, and winning by AIC is not the same as describing a real process (Cooper et al. 2016).

When α hits the limit of the range explored, the card prints “—” instead of a number and says that α ended up at the limit: a number there would be an invention.

The traitgram

The figure that puts the character and the tree in the same plane: time on one axis, the value of the character on the other, and the branches of the tree drawn between the reconstructed values. It shows at a glance whether there was convergence, and the tip labels are spread out so they do not overlap.

9.4How much phylogenetic signal there is

“Phylogenetic signal” is how much more relatives resemble each other than would be expected by chance. The two usual measures do not ask the same question, and that is why they can seem contradictory without being so.

Table 9.2. The two measures of phylogenetic signal.
MeasureWhat it manipulatesHow it is read
Pagel’s λThe shape of the tree: it multiplies the covariances between species without touching the variances.λ = 1 is pure Brownian motion; λ = 0, a character distributed as if the phylogeny did not exist. It is tested against 0 and against 1 with a likelihood ratio.

They are different questions about the same data: λ looks at the shape of the tree and K at the magnitude of the variance. A character can be distributed exactly as the tree predicts—λ = 1—and at the same time vary less among species than Brownian motion expected—K < 1. The panel explains this when it happens, because it is one of the most puzzling readings.

The plot of K under the null hypothesis draws the distribution of K when the values are shuffled among species, and places the observed one on it. Without that plot, a K of 0.8 does not tell you whether it is a lot or a little.

!Signal is pattern, not process

λ = 1 does not prove neutral drift, and a low λ may be due to measurement error and not to biology. With fewer than twenty species, moreover, both measures are estimated with very little precision.

9.5Two characters, one relationship

The panel fits three regressions on the same two characters and puts them side by side:

  1. The ordinary one, which counts each species as a new data point.
  2. Felsenstein’s independent contrasts: they turn the values of n species into n−1 differences between sister nodes, each scaled by its branch lengths. Those differences are independent, and their regression is fitted through the origin, because the sign of a contrast is arbitrary and an intercept would mean nothing.
  3. PGLS, which solves the same problem by another route: a generalized regression whose covariance matrix is that of the tree.
∑The Garland and Ives identity

Contrasts and PGLS are not two similar approximations: under Brownian motion they give exactly the same slope. The program checks this in the interface itself and shows it: the slope of the contrasts through the origin and that of PGLS with λ = 1 have to be the same number. When they are not, something is wrong, and it is a check worth looking at.

Comparing the p-value of the ordinary regression with that of PGLS shows, at a single glance, how much the significance was being exaggerated.

✕Believing that PGLS fixes other things

It corrects non-independence. It does not correct causality, an omitted variable or measurement error. And if the phylogenetic signal of the residual turns out to be nil, PGLS and least squares give the same thing: the correction does not always change the conclusion, and that too is a result.

9.6A discrete character: the Mk model

Lewis’s (2001) Mk model treats the states of a character the way a substitution model treats nucleotides: a matrix of transition rates between states. Three variants:

  • ER: a single rate for all changes.
  • SYM: the rate from i to j equal to that from j to i.
  • ARD: all different. With k states there are k(k−1) parameters: with four states, twelve.

They are compared by AIC. With few species ER almost always wins, and not because the biology is simple: because there are not enough data to estimate more.

✕Not checking whether the rate blew up

A rate that goes to infinity is the symptom of a saturated character: it has changed so many times that the tree no longer keeps any memory of its history. The nodes all come out at 0.5 and the reconstruction says nothing. That is not a failure of the program: it is the data saying they do not know.

iA convention worth knowing

The log-likelihood reported by ape::ace is that of phytools::fitMk plus log(k), and the whole difference is the flat prior at the root. It is not that one of them is wrong: they are two conventions. The program reports both, so you can compare with either package without surprises.

9.7Ancestral states and stochastic mapping

The pie charts

Each internal node carries a pie chart with the probability of each state. The version reported is the marginal one, which integrates over everything else in the tree. A pie that is almost a single color is a firm reconstruction; one that is split means that the data do not know, and that split is the result, not a defect of the drawing.

The program draws the pies on purpose, instead of writing the winning state: that way a node at 55% looks like what it is. Uncertainty grows toward the root, which is often exactly the node of interest.

Stochastic mapping

Instead of giving a probability per node, it draws complete histories of the character on the tree—where and when each change happened—consistent with the tips and with the model (Huelsenbeck et al. 2003). Five hundred histories in less than a second.

What is reported is the average over the histories: the expected number of changes, how much time the tree spent in each state, and how often each branch contains a change. That is considerably more informative than a single state per node: it turns “the ancestor was probably yellow” into “the lineage spent 70% of its history in yellow and there were about four changes.”

!The histories are only as good as the Mk that generates them

If the rates are badly estimated, all the histories will be off in the same direction, and the average does not reveal it. The spread among histories measures the uncertainty within the model, not that of the model.

  • The three continuous models reproduce the parameterization of geiger::fitContinuous.
iThree bugs the validation uncovered

Early burst lost all its digits when the parameter was small: the expression (ea·s−1)/a was computed as written, and for a tiny |a| that means subtracting two nearly equal numbers. It was replaced by the function that exists precisely for that.

The t distribution function came out symmetric about zero, because the incomplete beta only sees t². The p-values were correct by sheer luck, because it was always called with |t|.

The OU α was bounded between fixed numbers instead of numbers relative to the depth of the tree, and it once reported a half-life of 691,179 substitutions on a tree 0.08 deep. Now the limit is relative, and if α hits it, the card says so instead of printing the figure.

9.9Common mistakes

✕Publishing an ordinary regression across species

It still appears in journals. If the data are species and a phylogeny is available, the ordinary regression miscounts and its p-value is too small. The panel shows the three regressions together precisely so the difference can be seen.

✕Fitting OU on a non-ultrametric tree

OU and early burst assume that all tips are contemporaneous. On a phylogram, their parameters do not mean what they say. The block warns you; you have to read the warning.

✕Concluding “stabilizing selection” because OU won

With few species OU often wins for statistical reasons. Look at the half-life against the depth of the tree: if it is longer, OU is indistinguishable from Brownian motion and there is nothing to conclude.

✕Reporting the winning ancestral state without its probability

“The ancestor was yellow” is very different from “the ancestor was yellow with probability 0.55.” The pie charts are drawn so that this difference cannot be hidden, and the complete figure is what gets published.

✕Forgetting that the tree was pruned

Each analysis uses only the tips that have the character it needs. If out of eighty species only forty have seed size measured, your λ was estimated on forty. The panel says so; you should report it too.

10.1The character that splits in two

In Block 9 a discrete character changes along a branch and, on reaching a node, both daughters inherit whatever the parent had. A geographic range does not behave like that. If the ancestor lived on islands A, B and C and the lineage splits, the usual outcome is not that both daughters inherit all three islands: the usual outcome is that one keeps A and the other BC, or that one keeps everything and the other a single island.

That forces two things at once. First, the states of the model are not the areas but the subsets of areas. Second, two different processes have to be described: what happens to the range along a branch and what happens to it at the node.

Table 10.1. The two processes and their parameters.
WhenWhat happens
Along a branch
(anagenesis)		At rate d, the range gains an area: dispersal. At rate e, it loses one: local extinction. A single-area range that loses its area disappears, which is why the null range is a state, and an absorbing one.
At the node
(cladogenesis)		The ancestral range is divided between the two daughters. Which divisions are allowed is the only thing that distinguishes the three base models.
!First you need a tree, and it had better be dated

The dispersal and extinction rates are estimated per unit of time. On a phylogram measured in substitutions they do not mean what they say. The panel sends you to Block 7 if you do not have a chronogram yet, and rightly so: a d of 0.035 only means something if the axis of the tree is in millions of years.

10.2The areas and who lives in which

The program reads the LAGRANGE format, which is the same one BioGeoBEARS uses: a header line with the number of taxa, the number of areas and their names in parentheses, and then one row per species with zeros and ones.

It also accepts a comma-separated table with one column per area, which is easier to put together in a spreadsheet, and it exports in LAGRANGE format in case you want to take the same data to another program. The bundled example is of Bursera, and it lets you walk through the whole block without bringing anything of your own.

The names have to match the tips of the tree. The block says how many species it kept and which ones it did not find, instead of carrying on in silence.

10.3How many ranges a lineage can have

With n areas there are 2n subsets. With five areas that is 32 states; with eight, 256. The transition matrix grows with the square of that, and that is where the analysis becomes impossible.

The remedy is the maximum range size. If no living species occupies more than three areas, limiting the states to ranges of three areas or fewer cuts the problem down enormously.

!The cap is not a technical setting: it is an assumption

A lineage that was in five of six areas cannot be reconstructed if you set the cap at three. You have decided in advance that it did not happen. Often that is reasonable—very wide ranges are rare—but it is a biological decision, and it has to be reported along with the results.

The null range

It comes switched on. It is the state a lineage reaches when it loses its only area, and it is absorbing: there is no way out of it. Having it is what makes extinction a real process inside the model and not just a label.

The dispersal multipliers

The matrix of multipliers between areas is, of everything in the block, what most resembles doing real biogeography. Each cell multiplies the dispersal rate from one area to another: a 0 forbids it, a 0.1 makes it difficult, a 1 leaves it like the rest. That is where an isthmus that closed, a land bridge that opened or an ocean that was never crossed comes in. You edit it by clicking a cell, and there is one button to set both directions at once and another to put everything back to one.

iGeography that changes through time

The engine knows how to work with time strata—a branch that crosses a boundary gets the product of the matrices of the periods it passes through, each with its own multipliers and its own existing areas—but the interface of this block does not yet expose those controls. What you can edit today is a single matrix, constant over the whole tree.

10.4The six models

Three base models, each with and without +J. All three share d and e; what changes is which divisions they allow at the node.

Table 10.2. The three base models and which division they allow when a lineage splits.
ModelDivisions allowed
DEC

DIVALIKEOnly vicariance, but with any cut: the range is split into two complementary halves of whatever size. There is no subset sympatry.

iIf e comes out as zero

The panel spells it out: an extinction rate estimated at zero does not mean there was no extinction; it means that no trace of it was left in this tree. With small trees that is the norm.

10.5Why +J almost always wins

It is the most delicate point of the block, and the program does not hide it: whenever a +J model wins, the panel explains why that is to be expected and proves nothing on its own.

∑Ree and Sanmartín’s objection (2018)

The comparison is not clean. The parameter j buys probability at cladogenesis, which is an instantaneous event and costs nothing along the branches; d and e pay for theirs by integrating over the whole time of the tree. They are not competing on equal terms.

The fact that clinches the argument: in the cases they examined, +J won even on data simulated without any jump.

What can be stated, and what the block puts in the table, is by how much it wins and over which model. The likelihood ratio is reported with the warning that j lies on the boundary of the parameter space, and a p computed that way is conservative.

10.6Where each lineage was, and the corners

Once a model is chosen, the marginal ancestral ranges are reconstructed. Each node carries a pie with the ranges that have some probability; everything below the threshold—0.05 by default, and it can be changed—is lumped into a gray slice, so that the drawing does not become unreadable with thirty ranges of probability 0.001.

The corners

This is the part that many published figures leave out. A pie on the node says what range the ancestor had; it does not say what each daughter got, which is precisely the biogeographic event. The small boxes on the two daughter branches—the corners—show the range each one starts with immediately after the split.

✦The half of the story that usually gets lost

Knowing that the ancestor was in ABC does not tell you whether there was vicariance AB|C, subset sympatry ABC|A or a jump to D. That distinction is in the corners, and it is what gets told in the paper. They come switched on; leave them on.

10.7How many times each thing happened

One probability per node does not say how many dispersals there were or how many times a range was split. Biogeographic stochastic mapping draws complete histories—a range for every node, a division for every split and the moves along every branch—conditioned on what is seen at the tips, and by repeating it turns the reconstruction into counts of events, which is what a biogeographic history is made of. Five hundred replicates by default.

What is reported, averaged over the histories, is six quantities:

  • Dispersals and local extinctions along the branches.
  • The internal nodes divided among the four types of split: sympatry, subset sympatry, vicariance and founder event.

And with them, two warnings worth reading. If the standard deviation of the dispersals is large relative to their mean, the data do not pin down how many there were: only the order of magnitude. And if the sampling ran out of attempts on some branch, the panel says how many times that happened and warns that it slightly underestimates the counts—it tends to happen with long branches and many areas—instead of returning a clean number that would be wrong.

Everything comes out in four files: the model table, the ancestral ranges, the mapping events and the areas in LAGRANGE format.

And speed, three times over. Fitting one model took 7.4 s. 10.9Common mistakes

✕Concluding “there was long-distance dispersal” because +J won

It is the central mistake of this block, and that is why the panel warns about it on its own. +J almost always wins, even with data simulated without a single jump. Report by how much it wins and over which model; do not turn it into a biogeographic conclusion.

✕Setting a low range cap and not saying so

The cap decides in advance that no lineage was ever in more areas than that. It is a strong assumption and cheap to report: one sentence in the methods is enough.

✕Running it on a phylogram

d and e are rates per unit of time. On a tree in substitutions per site the numbers come out, but they do not mean dispersals per million years. Use the chronogram from Block 7.

✕Publishing only the node pies

The node says where the ancestor was; the corner says what each daughter got, which is the event. Without the corners, the figure cannot tell a vicariance from a subset sympatry.

✕Reading a spread-out pie as a failure

As in Block 9: a node with five ranges at 0.2 is the result. The gray slice is not noise in the way; it is the part of the range space that the data do not rule out.

✕Leaving every multiplier at one and calling it a model

With a flat matrix you are claiming that moving between two neighboring islands costs the same as crossing an ocean. Sometimes that is the null hypothesis you want; it is almost never the story you want to tell.

11.3The tanglegram, and its trap

Two trees facing each other with one line per tip. The lines cross exactly where the trees disagree, so the figure shows where the disagreement is and not just how much of it there is.

Before reading anything you have to untangle, and it comes switched on. Untangling means rotating branches around their nodes until the lines cross as little as possible. A rotation does not change either tree: it is the same topology drawn another way. Without that step, a tanglegram of two identical trees can look like a ball of yarn.

!A tanglegram with no crossings does not mean the trees are equal

It is the warning that almost no program prints, and the block carries it. A rotation can line up two different topologies: the lines come out parallel and the eye concludes “they are the same tree.” That is what the distances of the previous section are for. The tanglegram says where; the number says how much.

11.4Concordance is not support

Bootstrap support says how confidently those data hold up a branch. It does not say how many genes see it. They are two different questions, and with whole genomes the second one often matters more.

The concordance factors of Minh and colleagues (2020) answer the second one, branch by branch:

  • gCF: what percentage of the gene trees contains that branch.
  • sCF: what percentage of the alignment’s sites supports it, estimated on quartets drawn at random around the branch—a hundred per branch by default.

They are drawn as stacked bars against the one third that pure chance would give, which is the right reference when there are three possible resolutions, and as a scatter of gCF against sCF.

∑Well estimated and poorly supported at the same time

A branch with 100% bootstrap and 35% concordance is not a contradiction: it means that the data you have hold it up without hesitation and that two out of three genes see something else. It is well estimated and poorly supported at the same time, and both halves of that sentence have to appear in the paper. The block repeats it where needed.

iThe deepest branch

A branch that hangs directly from a bifurcating root has nothing “above”: the root is not a node of the unrooted tree. The four groups around the branch are then formed from the daughters of the sister. It is an easy case to overlook—and we overlooked it, as §11.8 tells—but today’s count handles it, and that is why the concordance table includes all the internal branches and not all but the deepest. It is worth looking at: the deepest branch is often the one that separates the two large groups of the study, that is, precisely the one discussed in the abstract of the paper.

11.5A species tree from the quartets

When genes disagree because of incomplete lineage sorting, concatenating them fixes nothing: it can give, with great confidence, a tree that no gene supports. The way out is to work with the quartets.

  1. For every group of four species there are three possible resolutions. The program counts how often each one appears among the gene trees.
  2. Each candidate tree gets a score: how many of the observed quartets it can explain.
  3. The best one is sought with a nearest-neighbor search, starting from the tree you choose.
!It is a heuristic, and it calls itself a heuristic

The search can get stuck in a local optimum. It is not the exact ASTRAL program, and the block does not pretend it is. For a paper on gene discordance, use ASTRAL; this is for seeing the problem, understanding it and comparing with what you already have.

11.6Drawing the conflict instead of resolving it

A consensus has to throw away the splits that contradict each other. A network keeps them all and draws them as boxes: the wider the box, the more weight the conflict carries. Two routes:

11.7The ABBA-BABA test

With the tree (((P1,P2),P3),O), a site where P2 and P3 share the derived state—ABBA—and another where P1 and P3 share it—BABA—are equally incongruent with that tree. And under incomplete lineage sorting they are equally likely.

That is the whole idea: incomplete lineage sorting is symmetric, and introgression is not. One of the two patterns being much more frequent than the other is the signature of hybridization. Patterson’s D measures that imbalance, and the block gives it a 95% interval by block jackknife—twenty blocks by default—because neighboring sites are not independent and a standard deviation computed site by site comes out too small.

You can test one particular trio or sweep all the trios against an outgroup.

!D ≠ 0 has three explanations and only one is introgression

The same imbalance is produced by a badly chosen outgroup, very unequal rates of evolution between P1 and P2, and an alignment error. All three have to be ruled out before writing “hybridization.” The panel says so every time, not once in the help.

Everything goes out in five files: the distance matrix, the concordance per branch, the splits of the network, the ABBA-BABA results and all the trees together in .tre.

iWhat turned up during validation

The weighted measures read half a branch. Every weighted comparison inherited the error: weighted RF came out as 2.54 where phangorn says 3.78. Now the lengths are accumulated per split, not read from a node.

The path difference counted the root. Measured in edges, the path between two tips depends on where the root is, and two identical trees rooted differently came out at 7.94 instead of 0. The measure belongs to the unrooted tree, so now the first thing done is to remove the root.

A tree rooted on a single tip grew one internal branch too many. The branch above its sister contains n−1 tips, and the test for “trivial” only looked at the lower side. One of the app’s own example trees showed 16 internal splits where there are 15, which inflates by one every comparison with a tree rooted elsewhere. The four groups around a branch were three below the root, for the reason given in §11.4: exactly one branch, always the deepest, was missing from every concordance table. Twelve branches where there are thirteen.

The circular decomposition had its four terms paired the wrong way round. The weight of a split is a four-point difference across the two gaps that bound it, and pairing the distances by gap instead of by end gives a number that looks like a weight and is not one: 91 splits where a sixteen-taxon tree has 29, and distances it could not reproduce.

And a small one with a big moral: opts.minSites || 20 turned a deliberate zero into twenty, so asking for the sweep of all the trios returned none.

11.9Common mistakes

✕Reporting a large Robinson–Foulds as “different histories”

Look at the quartet distance first. If that one is small, what you have is one unstable tip, not two conflicting hypotheses. Reporting both measures costs one line and avoids the wrong conclusion.

✕Reading a tanglegram with no crossings as proof of equality

A rotation can line up two different topologies. The tanglegram shows where they disagree; what says whether they disagree is the number.

✕Confusing concordance with support

100% bootstrap and 35% gCF describe different things, and both are true. Publishing only the first gives a very optimistic idea of what you know.

✕Concatenating everything when the genes really disagree

Under incomplete lineage sorting, concatenation can give, with very high confidence, a tree that no gene supports. That is where the quartets are needed.

✕Announcing hybridization with a significant D and nothing else

Rule out the outgroup, the unequal rates and the alignment first. A significant D is the beginning of the investigation, not its conclusion.

✕Presenting the quartet tree as if it were ASTRAL

It is a heuristic search and it can get stuck in a local optimum. Say so in the methods, or run ASTRAL.

!Nothing is drawn until you confirm that the photo is of that species

It is a deliberate checkbox and it cannot be skipped. A photograph hung on the wrong branch is a claim, not an ornament, and it is a claim the reader is going to believe without checking it.

iWhere they live and where they travel

The pictures are stored in the browser—in IndexedDB, separated by data set—and never leave your computer. On export, they travel inside the SVG, so the figure does not depend on any loose file that gets left behind when the paper is sent.

12.4Labels that do not overlap

The program does two things when labels get in each other’s way. The first is to separate them: push them along an axis until none is closer to another than the requested spacing, without leaving the frame. The second, when no more will fit, is to drop the least important ones and keep the ones that say the most.

✦And it says how many it dropped

That is the part that matters. Losing labels silently is worse than not drawing them: the reader does not know they are missing. The panel says how many it hid and where to find them—they are always in the table and in the package—and what to change if you want them all.

In the unrooted layout nothing prevents two tips from landing side by side, so there the ones that still collide are dropped and the most peripheral are kept, again with the count in view.

12.6The figure studio

Whatever is changed here applies to every figure in the twelve blocks, not just the tree: palette, font family and size, stroke width and background. The ten colors can be edited one by one—they are the ones used by the groups of the tree, the series of the curves, the states of a character and the areas of the biogeography—and separately there are those of the text, the axes and the support values.

∑Why one rule is enough

No figure in this program writes a literal color: they all draw through CSS properties, and their text through two or three classes. That was done so the figures would follow the light and the dark themes, and it has a second consequence that was worth exploiting: redefining those properties restyles the whole application at once. The studio writes a single rule, and no drawing module knows it exists. The rule is limited to svg elements on purpose: the colored labels of the interface use the same tokens, and a figure palette has no business recoloring the menus.

The nine palettes include the program’s own, Okabe–Ito (color-blind safe), viridis, earth, cool, warm, pastels, high contrast and grayscale, which is there for a very specific question: if the journal charges for color, can your figure still be understood?

The seven font families are all on any ordinary computer: none is downloaded from the network, because a figure that falls back to another typeface on the reviewer’s machine is a figure you did not design. The size goes from 0.7 to 1.6 times, the stroke from 0.5 to 3, and there are four backgrounds, among them transparent and white. One button puts everything back to the original.

iWhat is exported is what you see

An SVG loaded as an image has no style sheet, so on export the colors have to be resolved by hand. The studio hooks in there: its colors win over the theme, its typeface replaces the one written in the code, and the chosen background is written as a rectangle beneath everything. It was checked by exporting a tree and looking for the Okabe blue and the white background in the pixels of the PNG.

12.7The resolution the journal asks for

All the figures in the program are SVG, which has no resolution: it prints at any size without losing anything, and it is what you should send when the journal accepts it.

When it asks for a PNG at so many dots per inch, what it is really asking for is a specific number of pixels. The block does that arithmetic before generating anything and shows it: at 900 dpi a half-page figure is several thousand pixels on a side. And if the figure goes beyond what a browser can draw, it says so and explains why, instead of returning a blank image that looks like a bug in the program.

12.8The methods, written with the numbers of the run

The methods section is the part of the paper that lets somebody else reach the same result, and it is the one written last, from memory, weeks later. Here the program writes it from what actually ran:

  • A block that was not used leaves no paragraph.
  • A support measure that was not computed is not mentioned.
  • The number of replicates comes from the run, not from a template.
  • The bibliography is built from what was cited and nothing else: the program knows 52 works and only the ones it used appear.

You choose the language, the title and the authorship, and whether the figures are included. The report is a single self-contained HTML file: no style sheet to fetch, no typeface to download, no script to run. It displays in any browser and prints to PDF from there.

!An honest draft, not a finished methods section

What the program cannot write is why you chose what you chose: why that outgroup, why that fossil on that node, why that range cap. That is yours to add. The report saves you the parameters and the citations, which is where transcription errors are made, not the judgment.

12.9The reproducible package

One file with everything: the data as they came in, the trees in the three formats, the figures in SVG with their colors already resolved, the tables in CSV, the report, and a text file that says what each thing is and with what parameters it was made. You choose which parts to include.

✦The program writes the ZIP itself

Byte by byte, with no external library and no network. That is why it works the same with the app opened by double-clicking, with no server and no connection—which is how you are going to use it the day you need it on someone else’s computer.

12.10How well it does, measured

This block does not compute: it delivers. So it was validated by having R do what whoever receives the package would do—open it and read it with their own tools. 38 checks and not one disagreement.

  • The ZIP: R’s unzip() function lists and extracts every file, checking each CRC32 along the way, and the extracted sizes are the declared ones.
  • Newick and NEXUS: read by ape::read.tree and ape::read.nexus, the same 18 tips, topological distance 0 between the two and branch lengths identical to 10⁻⁶.
  • phyloXML: parsed by xml2; its tip names are those of the tree and its branch lengths add up to what the Newick adds up to (2.446035 against 2.446036).
  • The alignment: read by ape::read.dna as 18 × 1,711, with the taxa of the tree.
  • The report: a file with no <link>, no <script> and no <img>, with its figures as inline SVG; and, in a package built from a distance tree alone, citing no clock, no biogeography and no MCMC, because none of them ran.
  • The figures: well-formed XML with no unresolved var(--…), which in Illustrator would be a black line.
  • The CRC32 against the value that the ZIP specification itself gives for the string “123456789”: 0xCBF43926.
iWhat turned up during validation

The report module depended on the page’s translator, which broke it outside the app and, worse, produced a half-translated document: the fragments it kept pasting in—“non-parametric bootstrap (100 replicates),” “by outgroup,” “penalized likelihood”—were resolved once, in whatever language the interface was in, and then put into the Spanish paragraph and the English one. Now each fragment is stored as a pair, and the module no longer knows what language the page is in.

And one that is funny and instructive: the genome of a partition is stored as a two-letter code, which is reasonable in a menu and absurd in a methods section. It said “The genomes represented are: nu.”

The figure studio produced two more. The .art-ink class was used and had never been defined in the style sheet—the tip names of the chronogram fell back to black, invisible in the dark theme—; and the studio patched the export before the thing it was going to patch existed, because its file was loaded before the one it wraps. The test page uncovered it, since it exports without waiting for the document to be ready.

12.11Common mistakes

✕Publishing a cladogram and talking about it as a phylogram

Without lengths, the figure says nothing about how much change there was. If the text talks about long branches or accelerated evolution, the figure has to show them.

✕Raising the support threshold and not saying so

A tree from which the low support values have been erased looks much firmer than it is. Saying “support values ≥ 70 are shown” costs six words.

✕Coloring a paraphyletic group without warning

The panel tells you which ones are. A color in a figure reads as “lineage”; if it is not one, it has to be written in the caption.

✕Adding a photo without checking which species it is

That is why there is a checkbox that cannot be skipped. The photo will be the first thing the reader looks at and the last thing they doubt.

✕Sending a PNG when they accept SVG

SVG has no resolution and prints at any size. A PNG at 300 dpi that is then enlarged in layout reaches the printer blurred.

✕Handing in the report as it is

It brings the parameters and the right citations; it does not bring your reasons. Add why that outgroup, that fossil and that model before sending it.

✕Keeping only the figure

The package is small and contains the data as they came in, the trees in three formats and the index of what each thing is. It is what will save you when the reviewer asks, six months later, with what parameters it was made.

DGlossary

Alignment (alineamiento)
The hypothesis that the columns of a sequence matrix are homologous positions. It is not a neutral preliminary step: it is the first result of the study.
Ancestral state (estado ancestral)
The reconstructed value of a character at an internal node. It is reported as probabilities, not as a winner.
ASDSF
Average standard deviation of split frequencies between independent runs. It measures whether two chains arrived at the same place.
Bayes factor (factor de Bayes)
The ratio of the marginal likelihoods of two models. It compares by integrating over all the values of the parameters, not with a shortcut like the AIC.
Block jackknife (jackknife por bloques)
Error estimated by removing contiguous blocks of sites, because neighboring sites are not independent.
Blomberg’s K
Compares the observed variance of a character with the one Brownian motion predicts on that tree. It measures magnitude, not shape.
Bootstrap (arranque)
Resampling columns with replacement. How often a branch reappears is its bootstrap support.
Branch length (longitud de rama)
The expected amount of change per site along that branch. In a chronogram, time.
Brownian motion (movimiento browniano)
A model of pure drift for a continuous character: the variance between two species grows with the time they have been apart.
Calibration justification (ensayo de calibración)
Putting in writing which fossil, which event or which rate each age comes from, with its prior. It is the most debated part of a dating study.
Chronogram (cronograma)
A tree whose axis is time, not change. It requires a clock and at least one calibration.
Clade (clado)
An ancestor and all its descendants. A group that leaves any of them out is paraphyletic.
Cladogenesis (cladogénesis)
What happens at the node when a lineage splits. In biogeography it is where the range is divided between the daughters.
Cladogram (cladograma)
A tree drawn without branch lengths: it asserts relationship and nothing more.
Concordance (gCF, sCF)
What proportion of the gene trees, or of the sites, contains a branch. It is a question different from support.
Consensus (consenso)
A summary tree of a set of trees. Strict, majority-rule, semi-strict or greedy; all of them throw away what contradicts itself.
Convergence (of a chain)
The sampler is already drawing samples from the posterior distribution and not from the path toward it. It is diagnosed with ESS, PSRF and ASDSF, never with just one.
Convergence (phylogenetic)
Two lineages that reach the same state by separate routes. It is the source of homoplasy.
DEC
The dispersal–extinction–cladogenesis model for geographic ranges (Ree and Smith 2008).
Discrete gamma (gamma discreta)
Rate variation among sites divided into a few categories, with a single shape parameter α (Yang 1994).
Early burst (estallido temprano)
A model in which the rate of evolution of a character decays with time; the model of adaptive radiation.
ESS
Effective sample size: how many independent samples your chain is worth.
Founder event (+J) (salto fundador)
A daughter that, at the split, leaves for a single area outside the ancestor’s range (Matzke 2013).
Homoplasy (homoplasia)
Similarity that does not come from a common ancestor: convergence, parallelism or reversal.
Independent contrasts (contrastes independientes)
Differences between sister nodes, scaled by their branches, that turn n non-independent species into n−1 observations that are independent (Felsenstein 1985).
Introgression (introgresión)
The passage of genetic material between lineages through hybridization. It leaves an asymmetry that incomplete lineage sorting does not leave.
Maximum likelihood (máxima verosimilitud)
The criterion that picks the tree and parameters that make the observed data most probable under the model.
MCMC
Markov chain Monte Carlo: the algorithm with which the Bayesian posterior is sampled.
Mk
A k-state model for discrete characters (Lewis 2001); Mkv adds the correction for having sampled only variable characters.
Molecular clock (reloj molecular)
The assumption that change accumulates at a comparable rate across lineages. Strict if it is a single rate; relaxed if it varies from branch to branch.
Monophyletic (monofilético)
Said of a group that is a complete clade.
Null range (rango nulo)
The state of a lineage that has lost its last area. It is absorbing, and it is what makes extinction real in the model.
Ornstein–Uhlenbeck
A model with a force that pulls the character toward an optimum. Its α is read as a half-life against the depth of the tree.
Outgroup (exogrupo)
A taxon known to be outside the study group, used to place the root. A bad choice shifts the whole history.
Pagel’s λ
Scales the covariances between species without touching the variances. It measures shape, not magnitude.
Paraphyletic (parafilético)
A group that excludes some of the descendants of its common ancestor.
Parsimony (parsimonia)
The criterion that prefers the tree that requires the fewest changes.
Partition (partición)
A subset of columns with its own model: a gene, a codon position, a genome.
PGLS
Generalized least-squares regression whose covariance matrix is that of the tree. It gives the same slope as the contrasts under Brownian motion.
Phylogenetic signal (señal filogenética)
How much more relatives resemble each other than expected by chance. A pattern, not a process.
Phylogram (filograma)
A tree with branch lengths: it also asserts how much change there was.
Polytomy (politomía)
A node with more than two daughters. It almost always means “I don’t know in what order,” not “they split at the same time.”
Posterior
The probability of a branch given the data, the model and the prior. It is not on the same scale as a bootstrap support.
Prior
What is asserted before seeing the data. In dating, the shape that declares the uncertainty of an age.
PSRF
Potential scale reduction factor (R̂): it compares the variation within each chain with the variation between chains.
Pybus and Harvey’s γ
Compares where the nodes of a tree fall with where they would fall under a constant rate. Negative, slowdown; but missing species mimic it exactly.
Quartet (cuarteto)
A group of four tips. It has three possible resolutions, and counting them is the basis of the species tree and of a distance between trees.
Robinson–Foulds distance
The number of splits present in one tree and absent from the other. It breaks easily: one misplaced tip takes it to its maximum.
Saturation (saturación)
The state in which so many changes have piled up that the observed difference no longer grows with time.
Split (división)
The division of the tips into two sets produced by an internal branch. An unrooted tree is its set of splits.
Split network (red de divisiones)
A figure that keeps the incompatible splits instead of throwing them away, drawing them as boxes.
Stochastic mapping (mapeo estocástico)
Drawing complete histories of the character on the tree, conditioned on the tips. It turns probabilities into counts of events.
Support (apoyo)
A measure of how confidently these data hold up a branch. It does not say that the branch is true, nor how many genes see it.
Tanglegram (tanglegrama)
Two trees facing each other, joined tip to tip. No crossings does not mean they are equal.
Ultrametric (ultramétrico)
A tree in which all the tips lie at the same distance from the root. A chronogram of living species is one.
Vicariance (vicarianza)
The division of a range in two by a barrier. In DEC one of the daughters keeps a single area; in DIVALIKE the cut can be of any size.

GThe tool: license and how to cite it

G.1 · What it is and what it is not

PhylogenyPro is a program written entirely from scratch: it loads no third-party library, no file from a content delivery network, no minified code. Everything it does—the likelihood engine, the sampler, the drawings, the ZIP writer—is in its own code and can be read.

What it does contain from outside are published numerical constants: the twelve amino-acid matrices, the aligner’s BLOSUM62 matrix, the genetic code and the geological time scale. They are not anyone’s creative expression: they are the parameters of published scientific models, and the file PROCEDENCIA.md documents where each one comes from and how it was verified.

It is distributed under the GNU General Public License, version 3 or later (GPL-3.0-or-later). The full text is in the file LICENSE, at the root of the program.

iLicensing is not giving up authorship

Choosing a free license is a decision about what whoever receives the program may do. Authorship is neither transferred nor waived: the author’s moral rights are inalienable. You may use the program, study it, modify it and redistribute it; what the GPL asks in return is that, if you distribute a modified version, you distribute it with the same freedoms.

G.3 · How to cite it

At the root of the program there are a CITATION.cff file and a codemeta.json file with the complete, machine-readable details; many reference managers read them directly. The short form, for the program and for this manual:

∑Suggested citation

Barrera-Guzmán, L.Á. (2026). PhylogenyPro: molecular phylogenetics in the browser, from the alignment to the dated tree (version 1.4.0). Universidad Autónoma Chapingo. Zenodo. https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23005815

Barrera-Guzmán, L.Á. (2026). PhylogenyPro: User’s Manual (1st English ed.). Distributed with PhylogenyPro, version 1.4.0. https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23005815

That is the concept DOI: it always leads to the most recent version, and it is the one to cite. To pin down the exact version you ran—the right thing when the result has to be repeatable—take its own DOI from the Zenodo record; for 1.4.0 it is 10.5281/zenodo.23005816.

In the methods section, also cite the works behind the methods you used: the substitution model, the type of support, the dating method, the biogeographic model. The Block 12 report gathers them on its own, and only the ones your study actually used, which is exactly the list that has to go there.
