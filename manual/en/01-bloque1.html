1.1What a home page that computes is for

Almost every home page of a scientific program is a brochure: it says what the program does and invites you to begin. This one does something else. Besides presenting the route, it computes: two labs run real phylogenetics—exact probabilities, simulation, inference by three methods—every time you move a control. Nothing you see there is an animation.

The reason for that design is pedagogical, and it has to do with idea 3 of the introduction. You can warn a thousand times that “more data do not fix a wrong model,” and the warning slides off. Seeing, on your own screen, how parsimony recovers the false tree more often as you increase the number of sites does not slide off: that is not forgotten.

✦What is decided in this block

Nothing, technically: it produces no result that the other blocks use. What is decided here is what you expect from the study you are about to do and what you are going to believe from it. It is the only block worth reading in full before loading data, and the only one worth coming back to when a result does not add up.

The six cards in the header sum up the conditions you will work under: everything runs in the browser, your data never leave your computer, it accepts DNA, proteins, codons and morphology, it treats the chloroplast, the mitochondrion and the nucleus separately, it is in Spanish and English, and it exports figures at up to 900 dpi.

1.3Lab 1: the Felsenstein zone

This is probably the most important corner of the whole program. It illustrates the result of Felsenstein (1978) that founded the modern critique of parsimony, and it does so by computing, not by telling.

What it shows

A tree of four taxa with the shape that does harm: two long branches that are not sisters, separated by short branches. With four taxa there are only three possible unrooted topologies, and with four possible states per site there are 256 patterns; but by symmetry those 256 reduce to the 15 partitions of the set {A, B, C, D}, so the probability of each pattern under Jukes–Cantor can be computed exactly, without simulating anything.

The panel uses that exactness to answer the question with infinite data: which informative pattern is the most probable? If the pattern that gathers the most probability is the one that groups the two long branches, then parsimony, given enough sites, will choose the wrong tree with complete certainty. That is what being inconsistent means: converging to the false answer.

The controls

  • Long branches and Short branches: the two lengths that define the scenario, in substitutions per site.
  • Sites: how many sites each simulated alignment has.
  • Replicates: how many alignments are simulated to measure how often each method gets it right.
  • ↻ New replicates: simulates again with another seed.

What to read

Three things, in this order:

  1. The true tree and Where parsimony fails: the panel computes by bisection the boundary of the scenario, that is, the exact point where the long branch becomes too long and parsimony starts getting it wrong. Moving the sliders to one side and the other of that boundary is the whole lesson.
  2. The three informative patterns and their exact probability. When the “long branches together” pattern overtakes the true pattern, that is it: parsimony has lost, however much data you give it.
  3. How often each method recovered the true tree, over the simulated replicates, with parsimony, with neighbor joining on Jukes–Cantor-corrected distances, and with maximum likelihood optimizing the branch lengths on each of the three topologies.
!The experiment you must do

Set up a scenario inside the zone—long branches of 0.6 and short ones of 0.03, for example—and then raise the sites from 100 to 2,000. Likelihood improves, as you would expect. Parsimony gets worse: with more data it is right less often, because each new site reinforces the false signal. That is the difference between a consistent method and one that is not, and it is idea 3 of the introduction seen live.

The “Why this matters in your own data” panel translates it: a genus with one fast-evolving species, a very distant outgroup or a saturated marker produce exactly this geometry without anyone having looked for it. If your tree joins the outgroup with the most divergent taxon, suspect this before you suspect the biology.

1.4Lab 2: saturation and distance correction

The second lab makes idea 2 visible: a site that changed several times shows only the last change.

Two sequences diverge under Kimura’s two-parameter model, with rates that vary among sites according to a gamma distribution. The panel plots the estimated distance against the true distance, with three overlaid curves: the raw differences (the proportion of differing positions), the model correction, and the line of the perfect estimator, which is the diagonal.

What you see is immediate: the raw differences start out stuck to the diagonal and peel away more and more, until they flatten out. That is saturation. The correction brings them back to the diagonal, but only as far as it has a solution; beyond that, the formula breaks down and the panel shows it.

The controls

  • Maximum divergence: how far the two sequences are allowed to drift apart.
  • ts/tv ratio: how many transitions there are for each transversion. Raise it and you will see that transitions saturate much earlier.
  • Γ shape: the inequality of rates among sites. Low values—a few very fast sites and many almost frozen—bring saturation forward.
  • Sites: how many positions each sequence has; with few, noise dominates.
  • rate variation among sites (+Γ): turns the heterogeneity off and on to see how much it changes.

The second plot, Transitions and transversions, is the one you will use with your own data in Block 3. The rule can be read straight off the screen: when the transition curve stops rising and starts to fall, the marker is saturated. It is not a metaphor: accumulated transitions are lost because the sites change again.

iSaturated does not mean useless

A saturated marker still resolves recent nodes well; what you cannot do is use it for the deep nodes and believe its support. The usual solution is not to throw the gene away: it is to remove the third codon position, switch to amino acids, or look for a slower marker for the deep part of the tree.

✦Why the genome is not decoration

The chloroplast and the mitochondrion of a plant are inherited from a single parent—in most angiosperms, from the mother—and do not recombine: the whole genome is a single lineage, with a single history. The nucleus is inherited from both and does recombine. That is why a chloroplast tree and a nuclear tree can disagree without either of them being wrong: chloroplast capture after an ancient hybridization leaves exactly that signature. Declaring the genome of each partition in Block 2 is what lets the following blocks treat that disagreement as information and not as noise.

1.8The method gallery and the references

The gallery lists everything the application computes, grouped by family—data, models, quick trees, inference, time, macroevolution, biogeography, compare and publish—with a filter to see a single family. It serves two purposes: checking at a glance whether the method you need is there, and finding which block it lives in.

At the end of the home page there are 94 references, also grouped by family. They are not there for decoration: each method the application implements cites the article in which it was published, and the Block 12 report builds its bibliography by taking from that same list only what was actually used in your run.

iCite the method, not just the program

When you publish, cite the program and the original articles of the methods you used. An experienced reviewer will notice the difference between “program X was used” and “a GTR+Γ model (Yang 1994) was fitted with SPR search and ultrafast bootstrap support (Minh et al. 2013).” The methods section that Block 12 writes already comes with those citations in place.

1.10Frequent mistakes

✕Skipping the home page

It is the mistake everyone makes, including those who have been doing phylogenetics for years. The two labs take ten minutes and change the way you look at the results of the eleven blocks that follow. If you only have time for one, make it the Felsenstein zone.

✕Reading the table of limits as a promise

The times were measured on a specific computer with a specific alignment. Your machine and your data are different. The table tells you whether you are in the right order of magnitude, not when to set a deadline.

✕Concluding that “parsimony is bad”

Lab 1 does not show that. It shows that parsimony is inconsistent under a specific geometry of branches. Outside that geometry it behaves well, it is extremely fast, and for morphological matrices—where there is no credible substitution model—it remains the right tool. Chapter 4 returns to this.

✕Choosing the marker by lab habit

“We always use ITS” is not an argument. ITS is fast and resolves between close species, but it has divergent copies and incomplete concerted evolution, and for a deep node it will give a confident and wrong answer. The list of markers on the home page says, for each one, at what depth range it is informative.
