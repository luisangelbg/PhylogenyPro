Chapter seven · Block 7

The molecular clock and dating

A phylogram measures change; a chronogram measures time. Getting from one to the other needs three things that molecular data do not contain: a root, a model of how the rate varies, and at least one external calibration. This chapter goes through those three steps in order, explains the assistant that reviews calibrations before they are used, and ends by insisting on the one thing that must never be missing from a published date: its interval.

7.1What the clock cannot give you on its own

The branches of the tree produced by Blocks 5 and 6 are measured in substitutions per site. Not in years. A branch of 0.08 can be a million years of fast evolution or fifty of slow evolution, and the sequences, by themselves, cannot tell those two cases apart.

✦The confounding of rate and time

What a branch measures is rate × time. With one observed number and two unknowns, outside information is needed to separate them. That is the whole difficulty of molecular dating, and it explains why a date depends more on its calibrations than on any statistical refinement.

That is why the block asks for three things in this order: a root, because without it there is no direction and no ages; an answer to does the clock hold?, which decides which method to use; and at least one calibration, without which ages come out as proportions of the depth of the tree and not in years.

7.2The root

A tree without a root has no direction and therefore no ages. The root by menu offers four routes:

Table 7.1. The four ways of placing the root.
MethodWhen, and with what care
outgroupThe honest way when you have one. It is a hypothesis, and it can be wrong: an outgroup that is too distant introduces a long branch and can attract the most divergent taxon of the ingroup.
midpointPlaces the root at the center of the longest path. Convenient, but it assumes a clock—exactly what is about to be tested. Use it to explore, not to publish.
tip datesWhen the tips have different dates—viruses, ancient DNA, dated fossils—the data themselves say where the root goes. You write one date per taxon, one per line.
leave it as it isIf the tree already came rooted from another block or from a file.

The root-to-tip regression

With dates at the tips, the program plots the distance from the root to each tip against its date. If there is a clock, the two rise together in a straight line and the slope is the rate. The r² tells how much of the variation time explains.

!The r² of this regression is optimistic

The root-to-tip distances of two sister taxa are not independent: they share almost the whole path. That is why the r² comes out higher than it should and must not be used as a formal test of the clock. It is useful for looking, for spotting odd tips and for estimating an approximate rate.

7.3Does the clock hold?

The test compares two fits of the same tree: one with free branches, one length per branch, and another forced to be ultrametric, where all tips sit at the same height. The difference in log-likelihood, multiplied by two, is tested against a χ² with n−2 degrees of freedom.

A small p-value rejects the strict clock and calls for a relaxed clock.

✕Reading “not rejected” as “there is a clock”

It does not prove it. The test has little power with few taxa: with twelve species it rejects almost nothing. Conversely, with many sites it almost always rejects, because it detects tiny deviations of no practical importance. The panel says so in those words when the result is not significant.

7.4The calibrations

Without at least one, ages do not exist: substitutions per site and millions of years are two different scales and nothing links them. The program accepts four sources of years and, in the type menu, five prior shapes.

Table 7.2. The five prior shapes and what they assert.
ShapeWhat it says about the node
fixed ageIt has exactly this age, with no uncertainty. Almost never true in biology.
uniform intervalIt lies between these two ages, with no preferred point. The shape of a geological event.
normalIt lies around a mean, with a deviation (mean / M and sd / S), equally likely toward younger and older ages. The shape of an age taken from another study; never that of a fossil, because it would allow the node to be younger than the fossil.
offset lognormalIt is no younger than the offset, with a long tail backward. The shape of a fossil.
offset exponentialThe same, with the mass more concentrated near the offset.
!A fossil gives the minimum age, not the age

This is the idea to take away from this chapter. When that individual died, the lineage already existed, and it may have existed long before: what the fossil proves is that the node is no younger than it. That is why asymmetric, offset priors are used, which allow the node to be much older but not younger. Entering the fossil’s age as a fixed age asserts that the lineage arose exactly when that individual died, and squeezes the whole tree toward the present.

To select the node you choose the taxa of the clade—none means the root. If those taxa do not form a clade in the tree, the program says so instead of calibrating something other than what was asked.

7.5The calibration assistant

Above the calibration controls there is an assistant whose purpose is that nobody has to guess what M and S mean. It decides nothing: it tells you what you have just written.

It proposes the shape according to where the age comes from

You choose it in the age comes from and the program explains why that source calls for that shape:

Table 7.3. What the assistant proposes according to the source.
The age comes fromIt proposesBecause
A fossil assigned to this cladeoffset lognormalIt is a minimum bound, not a point.
A geological eventuniform intervalIt is a window with a beginning and an end.

It says, in years, what the prior asserts

This is the most useful part. “Offset lognormal, M = 1.6, S = 0.7, offset 20” means nothing to almost anyone; below the controls the translation appears, recalculated with every keystroke:

→What you read on screen

This prior says the node's age lies, with 95 % probability, between 21.3 and 39.5 Ma, with a median of 25.0 Ma, and cannot be younger than 20.0 Ma.

And it warns about the mistakes that ruin a dating

  • A fossil entered as a fixed age, or with a symmetric normal prior, which would allow a node younger than the fossil.
  • An offset of zero, which imposes no minimum age: it allows the node to be from yesterday.
  • A normal whose lower tail reaches into the future, that is, into negative ages.
  • A prior so wide it barely informs, or so narrow it feigns a certainty nobody has.
  • The confusion of crown and stem: the taxa you select calibrate the crown node—the most recent common ancestor of the group. If your fossil is sister to the group and not a member of it, what it calibrates is the stem, which is older, and for that you also have to select the sister group.
  • That this is going to be the only calibration, and therefore that the whole scale of the tree depends on it.
iWhy there is no fossil database

And there will not be one. Assigning a fossil to a node is a taxonomic judgment about that fossil’s characters and about which clade it is sister to: it is the part that is debated in papers and the part a reviewer examines. A lookup table would invite publishing a date that nobody looked at. The program helps with everything surrounding that judgment; the judgment is yours.

7.6The three dating methods

Three routes, from the fastest to the most honest about uncertainty.

Least squares

Fits the ages by minimizing a least-squares function on the branch lengths (To et al. 2016). It gives a point estimate in seconds, even with five hundred taxa. It is what you use to see the order of magnitude, or when the tree is too large for the others.

Penalized likelihood

Lets the rate change between branches but penalizes abrupt changes (Sanderson 2002). The smoothing λ parameter controls how much: a large λ approaches a strict clock, a small λ lets each branch have its own rate.

!λ is not chosen by eye

It is chosen by cross-validation, removing one branch at a time and seeing which λ predicts best. The choose it by cross-validation button does exactly that and plots the curve. If the minimum of that curve falls at one end of the range tried, the range was too narrow and has to be widened.

Bayesian relaxed clock

An uncorrelated lognormal clock (Drummond et al. 2006): each branch draws its rate from a lognormal, independently of its neighbor. It is the slowest and the only one that returns a distribution per node instead of a point, which is what should be published when calibrations are uncertain—that is, almost always.

7.7Reading the chronogram

The tiles summarize: the root age, the method, the computing time, the mean rate in substitutions per site per unit of time, and the rate variation, a coefficient of variation that says how far the tree departs from a strict clock: near 0, there practically was a clock; above 0.5, rates are very unequal and a strict clock would have given wrong dates.

The chronogram is drawn on the geological time scale—the bands of the International Chronostratigraphic Chart—with uncertainty bars at each node.

✕Publishing an age without its interval

A node dated at 30 Ma with an interval of 28 to 32 is well determined; the same 30 Ma with an interval of 8 to 70 says almost nothing, even though the mean looks just as round. An age without an interval is not a result. And the interval only captures the uncertainty within the model and the calibrations you gave: it does not include the error of having chosen a calibration badly, which is usually the largest of all.

Without any calibration, the panel fixes the root at 1 and warns that the ages are proportions of the depth of the tree, not years: they serve to compare nodes with each other, not to write “X million years ago.”

7.8How well it does, measured

Checked against ape and phangorn, with the results in the validation folder:

  • Rooting by tip dates reproduces ape::rtt: slope −0.00158106 against −0.00158106, with a difference of 3·10⁻⁹, and r² 0.783919 against 0.783919.
  • Penalized likelihood agrees with ape::chronos: over 15 clades, the maximum age difference is 0.0382 with the root at 1, and the correlation between the two series of ages is r² = 0.9906.
  • In the clock test, the program’s own ultrametric fit is better than that of optim.pml(optRooted = TRUE) by 11.8 units of log-likelihood on the same rooted tree, and both reject the clock.
iA bug the validation uncovered

The rooting search filtered by the sign of the slope, assuming that root-to-tip distance grows with the date. It grows with calendar dates and shrinks with ages counted backward, so the filter discarded all the correct rootings: it gave an r² of 0.006 where ape reaches 0.784. Now the sign only indicates which convention the dates follow, and the rate is the absolute value of the slope.

7.9Common mistakes

✕Using the midpoint and then testing the clock

Midpoint rooting assumes a clock. Testing the clock on a tree rooted that way is asking the method what the method already took for granted. With an outgroup, the test is legitimate.

✕A single calibration

The whole scale of the tree hangs on it, and its error propagates to every node with nothing to offset it. Two or three well-spread calibrations are much better than one, however good, because they also check each other.

✕Calibrating with a secondary age without widening its uncertainty

An age taken from another study carries the tree, the model and the calibrations of that work. Copying its interval as it is pretends that uncertainty does not exist. The assistant proposes a 12% deviation and recommends widening it, not reducing it.

✕Confusing crown age with stem age

The crown is the most recent common ancestor of the living species of the group; the stem, its divergence from the sister group, and it is always older. The diversification rates of Block 8 are computed with one or the other and give different results. You have to say which one was used.

✕Reporting another study’s rate as if it were data

A rate published for “the same marker” was estimated in another group, with other sampling and other calibrations. It is the largest source of error in a dating and the least reported.
