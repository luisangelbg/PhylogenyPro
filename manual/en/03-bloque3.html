Substitution models

A site that changed twice shows only the last change. This block deals with that: first it tests the assumptions that every model takes for granted—homogeneous composition and an unsaturated signal—, and then it fits 56 DNA models, twelve protein matrices or Mk and Mkv for morphology, and lets the information criteria choose. It is not a formality: the chosen model determines the branch lengths, the support and, in difficult cases, the topology itself.

3.1What is decided in this block

The differences observed between two sequences underestimate the changes that really happened, because a site that changed from A to G and then to T is counted as one change, not two, and if it went back to A none is counted at all. A substitution model is the tool that turns what is observed into an estimate of what happened.

Choosing it well matters as much as choosing the inference method. A model that is too simple underestimates long branches—and thereby feeds the long-branch attraction of Chapter 1—; one that is too complex spends parameters the data cannot estimate and increases variance without removing bias.

✦Why “always use GTR+I+G” is not enough

It is what many people do, and with thousands of sites it rarely goes wrong. But with little data GTR+I+G has eight free parameters plus the branch lengths, and estimating them all on two hundred positions produces very imprecise estimates. Besides, +I and +G compete to explain the same thing: together they tend to give extreme values of both that do not mean what they seem to. This block exists so that the choice is made by fit and not by habit.

The block works on one aligned partition at a time, chosen in the partition menu. If you have several, section 4 of the panel also looks for how to group them.

3.2What your data contain

The first section summarizes the partition before touching anything: how many taxa, how many sites, how many columns are constant, how many variable and how many informative, the G+C content and the proportion of gaps.

The sentence at the head of the section says something worth understanding well: constant sites tell you nothing about the topology but plenty about the model. A column where all taxa have A does not choose between topologies, but it does say that there were no changes at that site, and that is direct information about the rates and about the proportion of invariable sites. That is why removing constant sites “because they contribute nothing” biases branch lengths upward: the model believes there was more change than there was.

3.3The assumptions, put to the test

Before choosing a model, the block checks the two assumptions that no model on the list relaxes.

Composition per sequence

All the usual models assume that base composition is the same in every sequence and throughout the tree. The plot shows the composition of each sequence, and a χ² test compares each one against the mean.

!What to do if composition is heterogeneous

What does not work is changing the substitution model: none of the 56 fixes it, because they all share that assumption. Two sequences with similar composition attract each other in the tree even if they are not related, and the support will be high. There are three real ways out: removing the third codon position, which is usually the culprit; switching to amino acids, whose composition varies less; or recoding the nucleotides into purines and pyrimidines. The help card of the χ² test says so on screen as well.

Saturation

The second plot is the one you practiced in Lab 2 of Chapter 1, now on your data: transitions and transversions against the corrected distance. The rule reads the same way: as long as the cloud follows a straight line, the marker is informative; when it bends and flattens, it is saturated. Transitions saturate before transversions, and the third codon position before the first and second.

Remember what saturated means and what it does not mean: the marker still resolves recent nodes well; what it cannot do is support the deep nodes and their support values.

The quick set tries six (LG, WAG, JTT, cpREV, Dayhoff and VT) and the standard set all twelve. cpREV is there for a specific reason: it was estimated from chloroplast-encoded proteins, which is where most markers in plant systematics come from.

iWhere those twelve matrices come from

They are the matrices published in their original articles, each one cited in the code next to its numbers, and checked value by value against five independent canonical distributions—PAML, IQ-TREE, MrBayes, RAxML and the file that the WAG authors themselves deposited at the EBI. The PROCEDENCIA.md file documents the check.

For morphology, the model is Mk (Lewis 2001): a k-state model with all rates equal. Its variant Mkv adds the correction for the fact that morphological matrices almost never include constant characters—nobody codes the character “has cells”—, and without that correction branches come out inflated.

The controls

  • set: quick or standard, depending on how many candidates you want to try.
  • criterion: AIC, AICc or BIC, which is the one that sorts the table.
  • Γ categories: how many discrete classes the gamma is split into. Four is the usual number and what almost everyone reports.
  • starting tree: BIONJ, ML-optimised, neighbour joining or UPGMA. All candidates are fitted on the same tree, which is what makes the comparison fair.

3.5How the choice is made: AIC, AICc, BIC and weights

A model with more parameters always gives a better likelihood, even if the extra parameters are of no use at all. That is why the lnL is not compared on its own, but a quantity that rewards fit and penalizes parameters:

∑The three formulas

AIC = −2·lnL + 2k  ·  AICc = AIC + 2k(k+1)/(n−k−1)  ·  BIC = −2·lnL + k·ln(n)

where k is the number of parameters and n the number of sites. In all three, lower is better. AICc corrects for small samples and approaches AIC when n is large. BIC penalizes more heavily and therefore tends to choose simpler models.

What you read in the table is not the absolute value but the difference from the best, ΔAIC. Burnham and Anderson’s convention: below 2, the model is practically indistinguishable from the winner; between 4 and 10, clearly worse; above 10, discarded.

The Akaike weights column turns those differences into probabilities that sum to one: the weight of a model is the probability that it is the best of the set compared. A winner with a weight of 0.9 settles the matter; several models sharing the weight means that the data do not distinguish between them, and then it is worth checking that the tree does not change depending on which one is used.

!“The best of the set” is not “good”

If all the candidates are bad for these data—for example, because composition is heterogeneous and none of them models it—, the weight of 0.99 goes to the least bad. Choosing the model is not the analysis, and a GTR+Γ fitted on a crooked alignment is still a GTR+Γ fitted on a crooked alignment.

3.6Reading the winning model

Once the model is chosen, the program refits it properly, with all branches optimized, and shows its parts.

The rate matrix

The six exchangeability rates between pairs of bases and the four equilibrium frequencies. It is worth looking at the ratio between transitions and transversions: in nuclear and chloroplast DNA the normal value is around 2, and a value close to 1 in a gene that should have more is a sign of saturation—the transitions, which saturate first, have already been erased.

The shape of the gamma (α)

It describes how unequal the distribution of rates among sites is. Below 1 the heterogeneity is strong: a few very fast sites and many almost frozen. Above 3, rates are almost equal and +G adds little. The Rates among sites plot draws the discrete categories with their relative rate.

✕Interpreting α and the proportion of invariable sites separately in a +I+G

+I and +G explain the same phenomenon in two ways, and when they are fitted together they share the work unstably: it is common to get a high proportion of invariable sites and a low α, with neither value meaning what it seems to. Many authors recommend using +G alone. If you need to report I and α, do so stating that they come from a joint fit.

The verdict in words

Below the tables, the panel writes in prose which model won, by how much, and what its parameters mean. It is the text you can take almost as is into the methods section, and the one Block 12 reuses when it writes the report.

3.7Hierarchical likelihood ratio tests

The other way of choosing, older than the information criteria and still often requested by reviewers. It compares nested models—one is a special case of the other—with the statistic 2ΔlnL against a χ² with as many degrees of freedom as the difference in parameters, and chains the tests until it settles on the simplest model that is not rejected.

The program offers it because it is still asked for, and at the same time it says why information criteria are the preferred route today:

  • The result depends on the order in which the tests are chained, and there is no canonical order.
  • It only compares nested models, so it leaves out perfectly sensible comparisons.
  • The χ² is an asymptotic approximation and does not hold when a parameter sits on the boundary of its range, which is exactly the case of +I: the proportion of invariable sites is tested against zero, which is its limit.

3.8The partition scheme

With several partitions the question is not only which model, but how many different models are needed. Giving each gene its own is the most flexible and the most expensive in parameters; giving all of them a single one is the cheapest and may be false.

Find the scheme carries out a greedy merging in the style of PartitionFinder: it starts with one partition per gene, at each step tries the merger of two that improves the information criterion, and stops when no merger improves it. The result says which partitions share a model and which deserve their own.

With a gene split by codon position, the typical result is that the first and second positions merge and the third stays on its own, which is exactly what biology predicts.

The Export the chosen model button saves the scheme and the model to take them to another program or to keep a record.

3.9Frequent mistakes

✕Choosing the model without first looking at the assumptions

The two plots in section 2 of the panel are more important than the whole table of models. If composition is heterogeneous or the marker is saturated, the winning model will be the least bad of a list of unsuitable models, and its Akaike weight of 0.99 will say nothing about it.

✕Reporting only the name of the model

“GTR+I+G was used” is half the information. Missing are the criterion by which it was chosen, the number of gamma categories, and—if there were partitions—the scheme. The Block 12 report writes it out in full.

✕Removing constant sites

They tell you nothing about the topology, but they do about the model. Removing them inflates branch lengths. The only situation in which they are removed is when the model knows it and corrects for it, which is what Mkv does in morphology.

✕Believing that choosing the model well fixes the tree

It improves branch lengths, support and, in difficult cases, the topology. It does not fix a bad alignment, or a heterogeneous composition, or a taxon sampling that leaves a long branch on its own. The order of importance is: data, alignment, sampling, model.
