Data and alignment

The most important block in the program and the one that usually gets the least attention. Here the sequences are read, aligned, checked by hand, the doubtful columns are trimmed and the partition map is built, declaring which genome each partition comes from. Everything the following ten blocks do rests on what is decided in this one, and no later method can correct a mistake made here.

2.1Why this block decides the study

Aligning two columns means claiming that they descend from the same ancestral site. It is not data preparation: it is the first hypothesis of the work, and it is the only one that no later block can put to the test. The maximum likelihood of Block 5 will take your alignment as a fact and compute on it in all seriousness; if the columns are wrong, it will compute a false answer in all seriousness.

✦The consequence you need to understand

A bad alignment does not produce a tree with little support, which would be the honest result. It produces a firmly wrong tree: the misaligned columns become false informative sites, and those sites enthusiastically support groupings that do not exist. The bootstrap will back them, because the bootstrap measures the consistency of the signal, and the signal is there—it is just artificial.

That is why this block does not stop at aligning: it brings a viewer to look at the result column by column, an editor to correct it by hand, a trimming step with a preview and a quality control that looks for eleven specific problems. The sentence at the head of the alignment section on screen says it plainly: the eye of someone who knows the group often beats the algorithm.

2.2Loading the data: formats and what is guessed

Each file you load comes in as a partition: a gene, a region or a matrix. You can load several at once, by dragging them onto the box, choosing them with Choose files… or pasting the text with Paste sequences.

iWhat it guesses, it says

The program deduces the format and the data type—DNA, protein or morphology—and reports everything it had to guess. When a GenBank file forces it to take the names from the ORGANISM field, it warns you and asks you to check them before going on: that is where duplicate names and synonyms sneak in.

If you prefer to start without your own data, the four example buttons load the data sets described in Section I.7: Bursera · 3 genes, unaligned, Bursera · concatenated and aligned, Gene family · proteins and Morphological matrix. The three unaligned genes are the right choice for practicing this whole chapter, because they carry their true alignment stored with them and let you check how well you did.

2.3Partitions: the map of the study

The list of partitions is the map of what you are going to analyze. Each row shows the name, how many taxa and positions it has, whether it is aligned, and two menus: the genome and the data type.

!The genome is not decoration

Declaring whether a partition comes from the chloroplast, the mitochondrion, nuclear ribosomal DNA or the low-copy nucleus is the decision in this block that reaches furthest. The chloroplast and the mitochondrion are inherited from a single parent and do not recombine: the whole genome is one lineage with a single history. The nucleus is inherited from both and recombines. That declaration travels to all the following blocks, and it is what allows Block 11 to treat a disagreement between genes as information—chloroplast capture, hybridization—and not as noise to be averaged away.

Three buttons act on the whole set:

  • Concatenate all joins the aligned partitions into a single matrix, filling with missing data the taxa that are absent from any of them. The concatenated matrix keeps the map of where each gene starts and ends, which is what Block 3 will use for the partition scheme. You need at least two aligned partitions.
  • Split by codon position divides a coding gene into three partitions—first, second and third position—, which is what is done when the third is saturated and you want to model it separately or discard it.
  • Import a partition scheme… reads a scheme already written, in the style of RAxML’s.

2.4Aligning: the aligner and its options

The aligner is progressive, from the school that Clustal made standard and MUSCLE refined. It works in four steps:

  1. Quick distances between each pair of sequences from the k-mers they share, without aligning anything yet.
  2. Guide tree by UPGMA on those distances.
  3. Profile-to-profile alignment traveling the guide tree from the leaves to the root, with affine gaps (Gotoh 1982) and with the penalty reduced where the profile already has gaps.
  4. Iterative refinement, optional: each branch of the guide tree splits the sequences in two, the two sub-alignments are realigned and the result is kept only if it improves the sum-of-pairs score. It is the third stage of MUSCLE.

There are four controls: by codon, refine, gap open and extend. The last two are Gotoh’s penalties: opening a gap costs a lot, extending it costs little, because an insertion of six bases is a single event and not six.

iHow good it is

Measured against the true alignment of the simulated examples, the aligner recovers 99.98% of the pairs in rbcL, 98.14% in trnL-F—the one that carries indels—, 98.93% in ITS and 99.60% in the protein family. Aligning rbcL by codon, 100%. Those figures are in the validation folder and can be reproduced.

2.5Aligning by codon

If the partition is coding, this is the most useful checkbox in the whole block. With by codon checked, the program translates, aligns as protein and returns the gaps to the nucleotides three at a time.

The consequence is that a gap of one or two bases cannot appear, and such a gap, in a coding region, is almost always an artifact of the aligner: it would imply a frameshift that selection would have removed. Aligning by codon does not improve the result a little: it eliminates an entire class of error at the root, and that is why the rbcL example goes from 99.98% to 100%.

2.6The viewer: really looking at the alignment

The viewer draws the alignment on a canvas and copes with thousands of columns without slowing down. Three menus change what you see:

  • colour: by residue (each base or amino acid with its own), differences from consensus—which is the view that gives away a misaligned column, because the differences bunch up—or no colour.
  • mark variable and informative: flags which columns change and which are parsimony-informative. Remember that the informative ones are the ones that choose the topology; the rest do not.
  • codon positions: marks first, second and third. It shows at a glance whether the third is a mess, which is the signature of saturation.
!Ten minutes here are worth more than anything else

Go through the alignment from beginning to end with the coloring by differences from consensus. Look for regions where a single sequence deviates completely—usually a sequence in the wrong orientation, or from another gene—, blocks where the gaps are staggered for no reason, and ends with data from a single taxon. They are minutes that save weeks.

2.7Editing by hand

The editor has unlimited undo and redo, which is what makes it safe to experiment. There are eight operations:

Editing by hand is not cheating: it is what any experienced systematist does, because an algorithm does not know that this region is a microsatellite or that this taxon has a known duplication. What is mandatory is saying so in the methods, and the Block 12 report records that the alignment was edited.

2.8Trimming of ambiguous blocks

There are regions—highly variable spacers, poorly sequenced ends—where the alignment is simply not credible. Removing them reduces noise; removing too much erases exactly what is informative. The program offers two schools:

Gblocks (Castresana 2000)

It chooses by neighborhood rules: a column is kept if it and its neighbors meet certain requirements. Its parameters are conserved and highly conserved (how many sequences must agree), max contiguous (how many non-conserved columns in a row are tolerated), minimum block (the minimum length of a block that is kept) and gaps allowed, with three levels: none, up to half and all.

Per-column thresholds, in the style of trimAl

They decide column by column: by gap proportion, by column similarity, or gaps and similarity at once, with the max gaps and min similarity sliders.

Whatever the method, the program first shows what it is going to remove. The preview paints the doomed columns on the alignment, and nothing is touched until you click Apply the trimming; Clear the preview cancels. The keep whole codons checkbox forces whole codons to be removed, so as not to break the reading frame.

✕Always trimming, out of habit

Trimming does not always improve the tree. There are published studies where removing columns worsens the inference, because informative sites go out together with the noise. The honest check is to run the analysis with and without trimming and see whether the tree changes. If you lost more than half of the columns, the problem was not the noise: it was the alignment.

2.9Quality control: eleven things that are checked

The last section of the block looks for “what ruins an analysis without ever showing up in a spreadsheet.” These are the checks, with what each warning means:

Table 2.3. The quality-control checks.

Repeated namesTwo taxa with the same name break any later analysis, and the error shows up much later.
Names with awkward charactersBrackets, commas and accents that other programs reject: it matters if you are going to export to NEXUS or Newick.

Length a multiple of threeIf it is not, the last codon is incomplete.
Unequal G+C between sequencesThe subtlest warning and the most important: unequal composition breaks the assumption of almost all models, and makes two unrelated taxa attract each other in the tree. Block 3 puts it to a formal test.
✦The two warnings never to ignore

Internal stop codons and unequal G+C composition. The first almost always means something is wrong in the data—wrong frame, reversed sequence, pseudogene—and is easy to fix. The second is not fixed by changing substitution model: it is dealt with by removing the third codon position, switching to amino acids or recoding, and Chapter 3 explains why.

2.10Exporting, and what goes on to Block 3

The block writes FASTA, PHYLIP, NEXUS with its charsets, RAxML-style partition schemes and CSV. It serves both to save the work and to take it to another program.

What goes on to Block 3 is the set of aligned partitions, each with its genome, its data type and—if it is coding—its reading frame. If you come back to this block and change the alignment, the blocks that depended on it will tell you instead of going on showing an old result.

2.11Frequent mistakes

✕Aligning and moving on to the next block without looking

It is the most expensive mistake in the whole program, and the most common. The alignment is the only hypothesis that no later block can put to the test. Ten minutes in the viewer are worth more than any refinement of the model.

✕Not aligning a coding region by codon

It costs one checkbox and eliminates an entire class of error. If your partition is coding and you aligned it by nucleotide, go back and do it again.

✕Leaving the genome at its default value

The genome menus start with a guess based on the marker’s name. If your partition is called “gene1,” that guess will be bad. Correct it: Block 11 depends on it to tell a real biological conflict from an error.

✕Concatenating before looking at each gene separately

Concatenation averages. If two genes have different histories—which is exactly what this program can detect in Block 11—, concatenating first hides the finding. Align and check each partition on its own, and concatenate afterwards.

✕Confusing “many positions” with “much data”

A thousand positions of which thirty are parsimony-informative resolve little. The quality control says how many columns are constant and how many variable; that is the figure to look at, and the one worth reporting in the paper.
