Bayesian inference

Not “the best tree,” but the probability of each tree given the data and what was assumed before seeing them. That second term is what sets the method apart and what forces you to write down the assumptions that other methods leave implicit. This chapter explains the priors, the chain and its coupling, the four numbers that tell whether it converged, how posterior probabilities are read—they are not bootstrap values—and Bayes factors.

6.1What changes with respect to likelihood

Maximum likelihood asks: which tree makes these data most probable? Bayesian inference asks the opposite: how probable is each tree, given these data? To answer that, something more is needed: a prior distribution stating what was believed before looking at the data.

The result is not a tree but a distribution over trees, branch lengths and parameters. From it come the posterior probabilities of each clade and the credible intervals of each age, which is what makes this block the natural companion of Block 7.

✦The prior is not a weakness of the method: it is an obligation to write it down

Those who criticize the Bayesian approach for “depending on assumptions” tend to forget that likelihood also makes assumptions—the model, independence among sites, compositional homogeneity—it just does not force you to declare them. Here you have to write them down, and that is an advantage: the assumptions are in plain sight and can be discussed.

6.2What is assumed before seeing the data

The first section of the panel is the list of priors. The Use the model from Block 5 button copies the model already chosen; the next three fields set the priors:

  • mean branch length (exponential): the mean of the exponential on each branch length.
  • mean of α (exponential): the prior on the shape of the gamma.
  • mean of the rates (exponential): the prior on the rates of the substitution model.

In addition, the program uses a uniform prior on topologies—all equally probable before seeing the data—and a Dirichlet on the base frequencies.

!The branch-length prior is not harmless

It is the assumption that most quietly bends a result. With the exponential of mean 0.1—MrBayes’s default—and divergent data, the posterior of the total tree length comes out at half that of the maximum-likelihood tree: the prior pulls the branches down and wins. This is documented (Brown et al. 2010; Marshall 2010), and the panel warns about it before running, comparing the prior with the length that Block 5 measured.

The solution is not to remove the prior—that cannot be done—but to choose it deliberately and check that the result does not change with another reasonable one.

6.3The chain and its coupling

The sampler is Metropolis–Hastings over topologies, branch lengths and model parameters. At each step it proposes a change and accepts or rejects it according to a rule that guarantees that, in the long run, the frequency with which it visits each tree is that tree’s posterior probability.

There are six controls:

Table 6.1. The chain controls.
ControlWhat it is
generationsHow many steps each chain takes. More is better and slower.
burn-inThe first generations, while the chain climbs from where it was released, are thrown away.
sample everyHow many steps between saved samples. Saving all of them adds no information, because they are correlated.
runsHow many independent chains, started separately. With fewer than two, convergence cannot be checked.
chains per runThe coupled chains (MC³) of each run.
heatingHow much the posterior of the heated chains is “flattened.”
∑What coupling (MC³) is for

A single chain can get trapped on one peak of the posterior and never leave, because to reach another peak it would have to cross a valley of low probability. Coupling runs several chains at once: one cold chain, which is the one that samples, and several heated ones that see a flattened posterior and move much more freely. Every so often, two chains swap positions. This way the cold chain can jump to a peak the heated one found, without ceasing to sample the correct distribution.

Each run executes in its own browser thread, so four runs make use of four cores.

6.4Did it converge? The four numbers

The program puts the diagnostics before the tree, and not on a whim: a Bayesian tree from a chain that did not converge is a pretty picture of nothing.

Table 6.2. The four convergence numbers and their threshold.
NumberTargetWhat it says
ESS≥ 200How many independent samples the ones the chain took are worth. Ten thousand correlated samples may be worth thirty.
PSRF (R̂)< 1.01Compares the variance within each chain with the variance among independent chains. If all explore the same thing, it tends to 1.
ASDSF< 0.01How much two runs contradict each other on the support of each branch. It is the number MrBayes prints while running.
The trace—The plot of the log-likelihood against generation. Where it stops climbing and starts to oscillate, that is where burn-in ends.
✕Trusting a flat trace

A flat trace does not prove convergence: it may be a stuck chain that has spent a million generations without leaving a local optimum. That is why you need several independent runs and the PSRF, which compares among them. And the PSRF needs the runs to start at different places: if all of them start from the same tree, they can give 1.00 and all be equally wrong.

A high ESS is not enough on its own either: it says that the chain mixed well where it was, not that it covered the whole space.

6.5Posterior probabilities

The tree that is reported is the majority-rule consensus of the samples, with the frequency of each clade as its posterior probability. The conventional support threshold is 0.95.

!A posterior is not a bootstrap

They are different scales and are not compared. For the same branch, the posterior probability is usually considerably higher than the bootstrap: it is not that the Bayesian method “supports more,” it is that it answers another question. Moreover, the posterior is known to be overconfident when the model is misspecified: it can give 1.00 to a branch that the bootstrap supports at 60%. A 1.00 is not a guarantee; it is a statement conditional on the model and the priors.

The panel also shows the credible set of trees: how many distinct trees are needed to accumulate 95% of the posterior. If three are needed, the result is firm; if eight hundred are needed, the topology is far from resolved, however well supported the consensus may look on some branches.

6.6Bayes factors

AIC and BIC compare models with a shortcut: they penalize the number of parameters. The Bayes factor compares them by integrating over all possible values of those parameters according to the prior, which is more costly and more correct.

The quantity being integrated is the marginal likelihood, and the program estimates it by stepping stones (Xie et al. 2011): a series of intermediate distributions between the prior and the posterior, with the steps and generations per step controls. Never by the harmonic mean, which is notoriously bad.

Table 6.3. The Kass and Raftery scale on 2·ln(BF).
2·ln(BF)Evidence in favor of the better model
less than 2Not worth mentioning.
2 to 6Positive.
6 to 10Strong.
more than 10Very strong.
✕Comparing Bayes factors computed with different priors

The marginal likelihood is much more sensitive to the prior than the posterior, because it integrates over it. Two people with the same model and different priors can obtain opposite Bayes factors. And the estimate has its own error: repeating it with another seed tells you how much, and it is worth doing before publishing a 2·ln(BF) of 7.

6.7Taking the result away

The block exports the consensus tree with its posterior probabilities and a Tracer-compatible log, so the diagnostics can be reviewed with the tool the community uses. The tree passes on to Block 7 just like the maximum-likelihood one, and for dating it is often preferable, because the Bayesian relaxed clock gives a distribution per node instead of a point.

6.8How the sampler was checked to be correct

An MCMC sampler is hard to validate: there is no reference number to compare against, because the result is a distribution. This block was checked in three different ways, and all three are in the validation folder.

1 · Switching off the likelihood

If the likelihood is disconnected, the sampler must reproduce its own prior exactly, because there are no longer any data to pull it away. It is the most severe test it can be given, and it passes. In the recorded run—400,000 generations; the bench is stochastic, so another run gives different last decimals—:

  • The three unrooted topologies of four taxa come out at 0.3374, 0.3335 and 0.3292, against an expected 1/3.
  • With six taxa, all 105 possible topologies are visited, with a mean frequency of 0.00952, against 1/105 = 0.00952.
  • Branch lengths give a mean of 0.10060 against the exponential of mean 0.1, and the total tree length 0.50302 against the 0.5 of the corresponding Gamma(5, 0.1).

2 · Comparing the diagnostics with coda

The HPD and PSRF are computed on a series that R and the browser build in the same way, and they come out identical to those of coda, which is the reference implementation.

3 · Auditing the chain move by move

An audit compares the likelihood the chain keeps on record—computed incrementally, for speed—with a computation from scratch, at every move. The mismatch is zero in all of them.

iA finding only the sampler could uncover

During those tests the chain gave likelihoods thirty units above the supposed maximum of Block 5. It was not a bug: the chain estimates the base frequencies, while the maximum-likelihood fit kept them fixed at the empirical ones. That is where the +F checkbox of Block 5 came from, which now lets you estimate them there too.

6.9Common mistakes

✕Looking at the tree before the diagnostics

The panel puts them in that order on purpose. With an ESS below 200, a PSRF above 1.01 or an ASDSF above 0.01, you do not look at the tree: you lengthen the run.

✕Running a single chain

Without at least two independent runs there is no PSRF or ASDSF, and therefore no way of knowing whether it converged. A single chain with a beautiful trace may have been stuck for a million generations.

✕Reporting a posterior as if it were a bootstrap

“95% support” without saying which measure it is invites the reader to read it on the wrong scale. Write “posterior probability of 0.95,” and if there is also a bootstrap, give the two separately.

✕Leaving the default branch-length prior without looking at it

With divergent data, the exponential of mean 0.1 can halve the tree length. The panel compares the prior with what Block 5 measured and warns before running: that warning must be read.

✕Discarding 25% out of habit

Discarding a quarter of the generations is the convention, but looking at the trace is better than the convention. Sometimes burn-in ends at 2% and good data are being thrown away; sometimes it has not ended at 50%.
